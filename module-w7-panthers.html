Who Gets to Tell Your Story

Tuesday you saw the gap between how a group gets shown and how it would show itself. Today you build the theory of self-representation, then work the ethics of telling any story: consent, dignity, and who can even reach it.
📍 Thursday class meets on Zoom. Join at appstate.zoom.us/my/drsarahlong and sign in so you are marked present.
Move at your own pace, but do the whole thing: the reading, the calls you make, the case, and the writing are the instruction. Keep your Field Notebook open and your cause in mind. This case involves real violence and state repression; it's handled with care, and you can step away and come back if you need to.
Tuesday, in one line: the same free-breakfast program was "armed radicals recruiting children" in the hostile press and "the people feed their own" in the group's own paper. That group was the Black Panther Party, and the gap between those two headlines is why they built a newspaper. Today: the idea behind that, called self-representation, plus the ethics of doing it well.

1 · Self-representation: authoring your own image

When the papers and TV stations that reach millions describe you as a criminal, you can ask them nicely to be fairer, or you can build a channel you control and describe yourself. The Panthers chose the second. The Black Panther started in 1967 as a four-page newsletter and grew into the most-read Black paper in the country, more than 300,000 copies a week at its peak.

See it
Same week, two tellers. A mainstream paper runs a photo of armed Panthers on the courthouse steps under the word "militants." The Panthers' own paper runs a drawing of a mother getting a bag of groceries from the free breakfast and food programs, captioned as survival, dignity, self-defense. Neither is "neutral." The difference is who holds the pen. Self-representation is the act of taking that pen.
Read the real thing (7–9 min)

"The enduring influence of the Black Panther Party newspaper" (Columbia Journalism Review, free). Read it for two things: how the paper let the Party control its own story, and how selling the paper was itself the organizing.

"We feel that information is the raw material for new ideas. We have been deprived of information, we have been deprived of our history."
Your turn: the article notes that Panthers sold the paper on corners for a quarter and kept about half. Name one thing that daily act of selling gave the movement that simply publishing the words online never could.
Compare with one sharp reading
A strong answer names the relationship, not the reach. Example: "Every sale was a face-to-face conversation on a corner, so distribution doubled as recruitment, income, and a daily proof the Party was real and local. A link you scroll past leaves none of that behind." This is the Week 2 slow-media lesson in the wild.

2 · The image: drawing your people as heroes

The paper hit hardest through pictures, and one person drew most of them. Emory Douglas, the Party's Minister of Culture, designed nearly every issue for years. His technique was cheap and loud on purpose: thick black outlines, cut-and-paste collage, drawn so plainly that, he said, even a child could read it. He drew ordinary community members large and dignified, the way advertising and the news never drew poor Black families, and he drew the police as pigs.

See it
A weekly ad shows you a product and a life you can't afford. A weekly news broadcast shows you people who look like you in handcuffs. Now imagine opening your own paper every Saturday and seeing a woman from your block drawn like a hero on the cover. Douglas understood representation as a mirror: a community that keeps seeing itself as the villain will start to believe it, and a community that sees itself as the hero can act like one. "Art has relevancy," he said. "It's a language, that's the power of it."
Read the real thing (6–8 min)

"Designing a Revolutionary Visual Identity for the Black Panthers" (Hyperallergic, free). Read for how Douglas built a whole visual identity on a tiny budget, and what he meant by making art the community could actually read.

Then page through the actual paper: scanned issues of The Black Panther, 1967–1980 (open archive). Open any issue. Notice the covers, the drawings, the mix of hard news and open persuasion.

Your turn: pick one image you'd make for your cause's community, the way Douglas drew his. Who is in it, and what does seeing themselves that way give them?
Compare with one sharp reading
A strong answer centers a real person from the affected community as the subject, not the cause's logo or an outsider's pity shot. Example: "A drawing of the retired miners who show up to every meeting, drawn tall and unbothered, so 'the people fighting the water shutoff' have faces and dignity instead of being a sad-story prop."
Stay honest: the paper was community journalism and propaganda at once, open persuasion, and the Party said so. Drawing police as pigs dehumanized a group on purpose; it was a rhetorical weapon, and worth naming as one even when you understand why they reached for it. The Party also carried real internal contradictions, including sexism, some of which the paper surfaced and much of which it did not. And the threat they faced was real: the FBI's COINTELPRO program worked to destroy the Party and the paper by force and forgery. Self-representation is powerful, and it is still rhetoric. That is the honest frame.

3 · The representation call

Self-representation sounds simple, "tell your own story," but the ethics get hard fast. Telling a story can dignify people or use them. It can need someone's consent you didn't ask for. And it can shut out the very people it's about if they can't reach it. Two judgments per case. The obvious answer is sometimes wrong.

First, who is authoring the image? Self (the affected community, about itself), With (someone reporting alongside them, with their say), or For (an outsider telling their story about them). Second, what does it most do? Commit both, then Check. Note: "Self" is not automatically ethical, and "For" is not automatically wrong.
1. The Panthers photograph and write up their own free-breakfast program in their own paper. The parents and kids are named and quoted, with their permission.
Author
Does
Self, and it dignifies. The community controls the medium, the people are named agents, and consent is present. This is self-representation working the way Tuesday described it.
2. A national magazine runs a dramatic photo of a crying, hungry child from your neighborhood, no name, captioned "the face of poverty," to move faraway donors.
Author
Does
For, and it's extractive. An outsider uses a child's worst moment as a fundraising prop. The child is a symbol, not a person, and gets nothing back. Sympathy raised, dignity spent.
3. A well-meaning ally posts a survivor's story from your cause. It is moving and detailed and true. They never asked the survivor whether it could go public.
Author
Does
For, and the problem is the consent gap. Good intentions and true facts don't replace a yes. The survivor, not the ally, owns whether that story is public, and where.
4. Your cause makes a stunning photo-essay about Deaf members fighting for interpreter funding. You publish it as image-only graphics with the text baked into the pictures, and a video with no captions and no alt text.
Author
Does
Made with the community, yet inaccessible. A blind supporter's screen reader hits image-only graphics and reads nothing; the uncaptioned video shuts out the very Deaf members it's about. Beautiful and unreachable is still unreachable. Captions and alt text are not extras here; they're the whole point.
5. A journalist co-reports a feature with the tenants of a building. They review their own quotes, choose which photos run, and are named exactly as they wish.
Author
Does
With, and it dignifies. Here's the nuance: an outsider told this story, and it's still ethical, because the tenants held real control over quotes, images, and names. "Someone else wrote it" is not the same as extraction. Collaboration with consent can dignify too.
6. Your group livestreams a member's raw, tearful testimony at her most vulnerable to boost engagement. It goes viral. She later feels exposed and wishes she had never done it.
Author
Does
Self, and still extractive. This is the trap: it's your own group telling its own story, so it looks like clean self-representation. But trading a member's breakdown for reach uses her the same way the magazine used the child. Owning the medium doesn't excuse you from consent and care for your own people.

The pattern to carry: ask who authored it, whose consent it needed, and who can actually reach it. Self-representation is the goal, and it still has to be done with care and made accessible, or it fails the people it's for.

4 · The case close to home

Uptown Chicago, 1969 · the Appalachian tie

The Panthers' idea, tell your own story, build your own program, spread past their own community. In Chicago, poor white families who had migrated up from Appalachia looking for work were packed into the Uptown neighborhood, broke and looked down on. Some of their young people formed the Young Patriots Organization. Inspired directly by the Panthers, they wrote their own program modeled on the Party's Ten-Point Program, ran their own free breakfast and health clinic, and made their own media instead of waiting for the city to describe them.

In 1969 the Panther leader Fred Hampton brought them into a "Rainbow Coalition" with the Panthers and the Puerto Rican Young Lords, poor Black, white, and Latino organizers under one banner. Appalachian migrants and the Black Panther Party, telling a shared story on purpose. It's a real thread from this history straight back to these mountains, and a reminder that self-representation was a tool the Panthers taught others, not one they kept to themselves.

Your turn: the Young Patriots could have let Chicago's press define them as "hillbillies" and left it there. Instead they authored their own image. In one sentence, what did making their own media let them claim that borrowed coverage never would?
Compare with one sharp reading
"It let them define themselves as organizers and neighbors rather than a punchline, and set their own terms for who they stood with, so the story of poor Appalachian migrants got told by them instead of about them." Naming yourself is the first move away from being named.

Your cause

The real, low-traction cause you chose in Week 1 to carry through the whole course. Its obscurity is the point: your media work can actually move the needle.

Self-representation

A community authoring its own image, in a medium it controls, instead of being described by outsiders. The counter to being spoken about is speaking for yourself.

Example: when TV framed the Panthers as gunmen, they didn't beg for better coverage. They printed their own weekly paper and drew their own people as heroes. That move, from being described to describing yourself, is self-representation.

The Black Panther Party

A Black revolutionary organization founded in Oakland in 1966 by Huey P. Newton and Bobby Seale. Known for armed self-defense against police violence and, just as much, for "survival programs": free breakfasts, clinics, and schools.

About the Black Panther Party →

The Black Panther (newspaper)

The Party's official weekly, started April 1967 in Oakland. From a four-page newsletter it grew to more than 300,000 copies a week, the most-read Black newspaper in the country from 1968 to 1971. Sold on the street for a quarter; the seller kept about half.

Example: a single issue might carry hard reporting on a police shooting, a page of international news, and a full-page Emory Douglas drawing, all under the Party's own control.

About the newspaper →

Emory Douglas

Artist and the Party's Minister of Culture (born 1943). He designed and illustrated The Black Panther for years, building its whole look on a shoestring: bold black outlines, collage, and images plain enough that anyone could read them. In 2015 the design profession's main association gave him its medal.

Example: his covers drew mothers, children, and workers as dignified and strong, and drew the police as pigs, flipping who the community saw as hero and who as threat.

About Emory Douglas →

Free Breakfast for Children

A Party program begun in 1969 that fed hot breakfast to schoolkids for free, growing to dozens of cities. It was so effective at winning community loyalty that FBI Director J. Edgar Hoover called it the greatest single threat to the government's effort against the Party. Later public school breakfast programs echoed the model.

About the program →

The Ten-Point Program

The Party's 1966 founding platform, a numbered list of demands (jobs, housing, education, an end to police brutality) paired with beliefs. Short and plain on purpose, so anyone could read and repeat it.

Example: the Young Patriots in Chicago admired it enough to write their own point-by-point program modeled on it, adapting it for poor Appalachian migrants.

Read the Ten-Point Program →

COINTELPRO

A secret FBI program (1956–1971) that surveilled, infiltrated, and disrupted domestic political groups, with the Black Panther Party a top target. Tactics included forged letters to turn allies against each other, raids, and worse. It is why the "threat" the Party faced was real and documented, not paranoia.

What COINTELPRO was →

Propaganda

Communication made to persuade toward a cause, openly taking a side rather than aiming for neutral report. It isn't automatically false; it's information shaped to move you.

Example: The Black Panther mixed real reporting with open advocacy for the Party. Calling it propaganda isn't an insult here; it's an accurate description the Party would have accepted. The skill is reading any source, including a sympathetic one, knowing what it wants from you.

Extractive imagery ("poverty porn")

Using a person's suffering, usually a poor or marginalized person's, as a spectacle to move an outside audience, while giving the subject no voice, no name, and nothing back.

Example: a charity ad with a nameless crying child captioned "the face of hunger." It raises money by spending the child's dignity. The fix is to let people speak for themselves, with names and consent, as agents rather than props.

Alt text

A short written description attached to an image so that people using screen readers (many blind and low-vision users) know what the picture shows. Without it, the image is silence.

Example: a photo of a packed rally gets alt text like "Roughly 200 people fill the courthouse steps holding hand-lettered signs." A screen reader speaks that aloud, so the image reaches everyone, including blind and low-vision users.

How to write good alt text (WebAIM) →

Captions

On-screen text of what's spoken (and important sounds) in a video, so Deaf and hard-of-hearing viewers can follow it, along with anyone watching with the sound off.

Example: a two-minute testimony video with burned-in or uploaded captions reaches a Deaf viewer, a commuter on a silent phone, and a non-native speaker. No captions, and all three are shut out of your own message.

Young Patriots Organization

A group of young, poor white migrants from Appalachia living in Uptown, Chicago, active around 1968–1973. Inspired by the Black Panthers, they ran a free breakfast and a health clinic and organized their neighbors against slum landlords and police abuse.

A little history + the Appalachian tie: these were families who had left the coalfields and hollers of Kentucky, West Virginia, and Tennessee for factory jobs up north, and got branded "hillbillies" and shut out. Some wore the Confederate flag at first, then dropped it as their politics changed. Their story is a direct line from these mountains into one of the boldest coalitions of the era, and proof that self-representation was a tool poor Appalachians picked up too.

Fred Hampton & the Rainbow Coalition

Fred Hampton (1948–1969) was the young, magnetic chairman of the Illinois Black Panthers. In 1969 he built a "Rainbow Coalition" uniting the Panthers, the Puerto Rican Young Lords, and the Appalachian Young Patriots around shared class struggle across race. He was killed at 21 in a pre-dawn police raid tied to COINTELPRO.
