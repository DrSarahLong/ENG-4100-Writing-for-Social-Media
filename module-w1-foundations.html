1 · Communication as transmission, and as ritual

In 1975, James Carey pointed out that we secretly carry two different definitions of the word "communication," and we usually only notice one. The first he called the transmission view: communication as sending, moving a message across space to reach people and, often, to control distance and influence them. The second is the ritual view: communication as gathering, the drawing of people together into a shared world, a common faith, a sense of belonging. Look inside the word and you find its cousins hiding there: communion, community, common.

See it: same act, two views
Take a small-town newspaper. The transmission reading: it delivers information (the school board voted, rain is coming, the diner changed hands) to readers spread across the county. The ritual reading: opening that same paper over coffee is a small daily ceremony that says "I am part of this place," a shared world reaffirmed. Carey's point is that we obsess over the first reading and barely notice the second, yet the second is where community actually lives.
Read the real thing (6–8 min)

Carey, "A Cultural Approach to Communication" (Michigan Tech, free PDF). Read the first several pages, up through where he lays out the two views. He writes in long, careful sentences, so read slowly. Watch for the moment he ties "communication" to "communion."

"The ritual view of communication is directed not toward the extension of messages in space but toward the maintenance of society in time; not the act of imparting information but the representation of shared beliefs."
Your turn: name one thing you do every day that looks like pure transmission (just sending or getting info) but is secretly also a ritual (it keeps some little world of yours intact). What shared world does it maintain?
Compare with one sharp reading
A strong answer separates the information from the belonging. Example: "Checking my roommates' 'leaving now' texts is transmission on the surface, logistics. Underneath, the ping is ritual: it says we still move through the day as a unit. If they stopped, I'd feel it long before I missed the info."

2 · The "social" is a skill, and it's been thinning out

Here's why Carey's ritual view matters for this course. The gathering kind of communication, the kind that builds social capital, has been quietly shrinking in American life for decades, and it started well before smartphones. In 1995, the political scientist Robert Putnam noticed something strange in the data: more Americans were bowling than ever, but bowling leagues were collapsing. People were bowling alone. He used it as an image for a much larger pattern, fewer clubs, fewer quilting bees and lodges and PTA meetings, less of the face-to-face ritual life that holds a community together.

See it: what "bowling alone" means
Two towns, same number of bowlers. In Town A they bowl in leagues: same faces every Thursday, they learn each other's kids' names, and when someone's roof caves in, forty people already know and show up. In Town B everyone bowls solo, scrolling between frames. Identical activity, opposite social capital. Putnam's warning: we have been drifting from Town A toward Town B for fifty years, and a place with no ritual life has no one to call when the roof caves in.
Read the real thing (6–8 min)

Putnam, "Bowling Alone: America's Declining Social Capital" (open archive, free PDF). Read the opening and the section on the decline of clubs and associations. Ignore the 1990s references; the pattern is the point.

Your turn: name one real place ritual life has thinned in your own world (your hometown, your family, your dorm, this campus). What used to gather people there, and what replaced it?
Compare with one sharp reading
A strong answer names the specific ritual and what took its place, not a vague "people are on their phones." Example: "The neighborhood I grew up in had a July block party every year, thirty houses, one street closed. It died when a Facebook group 'replaced' it. The group has 200 members and we've met zero times. Reach went up; the block went cold."
Stay honest: the phone is not the villain here, and this course is not going to tell you to throw it in a creek. Putnam was writing before smartphones, so the drift is older and bigger than any one device. And digital media can absolutely build ritual (you'll test that in a second, and you'll design it for your cause). The claim is narrower and truer: gathering people into a shared world is a real skill, most of this generation got very little practice at it, and it can be rebuilt on purpose, phone included.

3 · Transmission or ritual?

Two judgments per case, and the obvious answer is sometimes wrong. First, does the act lean transmission, ritual, or a genuine mix? Second, what is it really doing underneath? Commit both, then Check. Watch for acts that feel social but only transmit, and acts that look like "just an app" but do real ritual work.
1. A county emergency system pushes a flash-flood alert to every phone in the area at 2 a.m.
Leans
Doing
Transmission, pure and simple. It moves urgent information across space to reach everyone fast. No shared world forms, and that's fine: this is exactly the job transmission is built for. A warm-up.
2. The same dozen neighbors hold a monthly covered-dish supper, phones in a basket by the door.
Leans
Doing
Ritual, about as pure as it gets. Almost no new information changes hands, yet the shared world gets rebuilt every month. This is Carey's ritual view you could eat off of.
3. After a tragedy in the news, you repost a plain black square. It gets 300 likes, mostly from people you've never met.
Leans
Doing
The tempting answer is "ritual," it feels like solidarity. Look closer: it leans transmission wearing ritual's clothes. A signal broadcasts outward, nobody is gathered, no shared world is maintained past the scroll. The feeling of communion without the thing itself. This is the trap the whole course is watching for.
4. In 1969, a whole country stops and watches the same live moon-landing broadcast at the same hour.
Leans
Doing
Mixed, and this is the one people miss. Yes it transmits (here is the news, a man is on the moon). But everyone experiencing it together, in the same hour, turns a broadcast into a shared national moment. Carey's real insight: the same act can be read both ways at once, and the ritual layer is the one we forget to see.
5. A members-only Discord of 30 volunteers who plan, argue, joke, and check in on each other every single day.
Leans
Doing
Ritual, on a screen. The easy answer is "it's an app, so transmission," and that's wrong. Thirty people who show up daily, know each other's moods, and maintain a shared world are doing exactly what the covered-dish supper does. The medium is digital; the work is ritual. Remember this when you build your cause's channels.
6. A GoFundMe for a neighbor's cancer bills raises money from far-off strangers, and the whole town starts dropping off casseroles.
Leans
Doing
Mixed, and a hopeful one. The page transmits an ask across huge distance (strangers send money). At the same time it pulls a real, local community into motion (the casseroles, the visits). The best cause media often runs both layers at once: reach to strangers, ritual for the people close by.

4 · A case built almost entirely out of ritual

The Tennessee mountains, before the marches

Before the buses, before the famous speeches, there was a place up on the Cumberland Plateau in Tennessee called the Highlander Folk School. Founded in 1932, it had almost no "reach." What it had was ritual: Black and white organizers, in the segregated South, eating at the same table, sleeping under the same roof, singing together, and working through problems in workshops. No broadcast. No audience. Just people gathered into a shared world that the world outside said could not exist.

Rosa Parks attended a Highlander workshop a few months before she refused to give up her seat. The song "We Shall Overcome" was shaped and spread there. None of that is transmission. It is Carey's ritual view working as movement infrastructure: the trust and the courage got built in the gathering, years before there was anything to broadcast. And it happened in these mountains.

Your turn: Highlander had no reach tools at all and still helped seed a movement. In one sentence, what did all that slow, in-person ritual give people that a viral post never could?
Compare with one sharp reading
"It gave them a rehearsed, trusting community, people who had already eaten together and sung together, so that when the risk came, they weren't strangers deciding whether to be brave; they were a we that had already decided." Ritual builds the trust that makes hard, sustained action possible. Reach can't manufacture that.
Handle with care: Highlander's story sits inside the violence of segregation, and the people in it took real risks. The tidy "We Shall Overcome" origin is a little messier than the one-line version (the song passed through Black church tradition, tobacco-workers' picket lines, and musicians like Zilphia Horton, Guy Carawan, and Pete Seeger before it became the anthem). The truth to hold: the anthem, like the movement, was made in ritual, hand to hand and voice to voice, not broadcast into being.

James W. Carey

American communication scholar (1934–2006), often called the father of "cultural studies" in U.S. communication. His famous move: splitting communication into a transmission view and a ritual view.

Example: he argued that reading a newspaper isn't mainly about getting news (transmission); it's a daily ritual that reaffirms you belong to a world.

Who Carey was →

Transmission view

Communication as sending: moving a message across space to reach people, spread information, and often to influence or control. Strong at reach.

Example: a weather alert pushed to a million phones, or a company blasting the same ad to a whole state. Information travels; no community necessarily forms.

Ritual view

Communication as gathering: drawing people together into a shared world, a common meaning, a sense of belonging. Strong at trust. This is the "social" the course rebuilds.

Example: a Sunday supper, a singing, a standing weekly meetup. Little new information changes hands, but the community gets remade every time.

Ritual vs. transmission →

Communion, community, common

All three share a root with "communication." To communicate first meant to make something common, to hold it together, not merely to send it.

Example: "communion" isn't the delivery of a wafer; it's people made one by a shared act. Carey wants you to hear that older meaning inside the word.

Social capital

The value that lives in relationships: the networks, trust, and habits of helping each other that a group builds up over time. You can't see it, but you feel its absence.

Example: in a street full of neighbors who know each other, a lost dog is home by dinner. Same street full of strangers, it's gone. Same houses, different social capital.

What social capital is →

Robert Putnam

American political scientist (born 1941). His 1995 essay "Bowling Alone" argued that Americans had been drifting out of clubs, leagues, and civic groups for decades, thinning the country's social capital.

Example: his signature image, more people bowling than ever, but bowling leagues collapsing, "bowling alone," became shorthand for a whole society doing that.

Quilting bee

A gathering where people (usually women) quilted together, one big shared job done by many hands, with the talk and the company as the real point.

A little history: "bees" (quilting bees, spelling bees, husking bees) were how rural communities turned necessary work into social occasions, so the labor got done and the bonds got built at the same time.

Why it's in this course: in Appalachia the quilting bee (and the "quilting frame" lowered from the ceiling) was a core social medium, news, courtship, grief, and mutual aid all happened over the stitching. High-trust, no-screen ritual: exactly the "social" we're putting back.

What a quilting bee was →

Highlander Folk School

A training center founded in 1932 in the Tennessee mountains (later the Highlander Research and Education Center). It brought organizers together across the color line for workshops, meals, and singing, decades before "influence" meant follower counts.

Example: Rosa Parks trained there months before the Montgomery Bus Boycott. Its power was ritual, people gathered and changed by being together, not broadcast reach.

"We Shall Overcome"

The anthem of the U.S. civil rights movement. It came up out of Black church tradition and labor picket lines, was shaped at Highlander (Zilphia Horton, then Guy Carawan and Pete Seeger), and spread the way ritual media spread: sung, hand to hand, not broadcast.

Discord

A chat app built around private "servers," group spaces split into topic channels for text, voice, and video. Started with gamers; now runs everything from study groups to organizing hubs.

Example: a mutual-aid group might keep a members-only Discord where 30 volunteers coordinate daily, invisible to the public and the algorithm. Digital, but the work inside it can be pure ritual.

What Discord is →

Singing convention (Sacred Harp)

An all-day gathering where people sing four-part hymns from "shape-note" books, arranged in a hollow square facing inward, taking turns leading. It's participatory, not a performance: there's no audience, everyone sings.

A little history: shape-note singing spread through 1800s singing schools and became a fixture of Southern rural life, often followed by, of course, dinner on the grounds.

Why it's in this course: Sacred Harp is alive across Southern Appalachia. It's a near-perfect ritual medium, no reach, total participation, and a shared world built by voices in a room. You can't stream your way into it; you have to show up and sing.

What Sacred Harp is →

Broadside

A single sheet of paper printed on one side, tacked to a post or a wall or handed out in the street, the poster/flyer of earlier centuries. Cheap, fast, public.

Example: a colonial town might wake up to a broadside nailed to the tavern door announcing a meeting or denouncing a tax. It was a way to reach a whole town before newspapers were common.

What a broadside was →

"The medium is the message"

Marshall McLuhan's 1964 claim that the biggest effect of any medium isn't the content it carries but how the medium itself reshapes how we think and relate. Next week's starting point.

Example: a text and a handwritten letter can carry the identical words and still mean completely different things, because the medium is doing its own talking.
