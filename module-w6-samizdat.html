1 · How samizdat actually held together

Start with the mechanism, because it explains everything else. In the USSR the State owned the presses, so a banned text spread the only way left: a reader retyped it on a home typewriter, laid in carbon paper to make a few copies at once, and passed those to people they trusted, who retyped it again. No headquarters. No mailing list. The phrase for it is exact: the reader is the publisher. The same tradition of slow, hand-to-hand circulation shows up closer to home too, in the mountain ballad world of Southern Appalachia.

See it
A poem you could go to prison for holding reaches ten thousand people, and there is no press to raid, no server to seize, no subscriber list to demand. To stop it, the KGB would have to find and silence every reader, and every reader is also a printer. The constraint (no copying allowed) did not shrink the network into nothing. It reshaped it into something the State could not grab: decentralized, personal, and slow.
Read the real thing (7–9 min)

"Samizdat in Samara," a firsthand account by Valery Pavlukevich (openDemocracy, free full text). A dissident remembers the underground book world of his provincial city: the coded phone calls, the risk, the typewriters that had to be kept off the KGB's radar.

"It was dangerous to try to photocopy anything by Solzhenitsyn. A mass run of 100 copies could land you in prison." (Pavlukevich describes how even electric typewriters registered with the KGB were avoided, and pre-revolutionary machines were prized because the police had no sample of their type.)
Your turn: the account is full of small, careful choices (which typewriter, which words on the phone, whom to trust). Pick one and say what it reveals about how surveillance shapes a movement's media, even before anyone is arrested.
Compare with one sharp reading
A strong answer connects a tiny craft choice to the politics around it. Example: "The coded word 'chocolates' for banned books shows the movement had to build a private language just to talk about its own media. Surveillance doesn't only punish speech after the fact; it reaches back and reshapes how people can even ask for a book."
The callback
Two weeks ago Langdon Winner argued that artifacts have politics. Samizdat is the cleanest case you will meet. A printing press is central, costly, and seizable. A typewriter with carbon paper is none of those, so the tool's very limits (slow, by hand, a few copies at a time) forced a decentralized network with no center to seize. The politics were poured into the object. The weakness became the security feature.

2 · What surveillance does before it arrests anyone

Here is the turn from history to your own work. Samizdat existed because people were watched. You are not facing the KGB, but your cause still lives under watchful systems: platforms that log everything, employers who search names, opponents who screenshot. The most important effect of being watched is not arrest. It is what people stop doing on their own. Researchers call it the chilling effect, and it works through quiet self-censorship.

See it
After the 2013 revelations that the NSA was collecting bulk internet data, traffic to Wikipedia articles on terms like "car bomb" and "Al Qaeda" dropped measurably. Nobody was arrested for reading them. People simply stopped, because they felt watched. That is the chilling effect: surveillance changes behavior without ever charging a soul. For a movement, the danger is that your most vulnerable members go quiet before you even ask them to speak.
Read the real thing (8–10 min)

Jonathon Penney, "Internet surveillance, regulation, and chilling effects online" (Internet Policy Review, open access, CC-BY). Read the introduction and the findings. Penney surveyed over 1,200 people; skim for the numbers on who self-censors most.

Penney found that a personal legal threat made about 75% of people say they would speak less online, and awareness of government surveillance chilled about 62%. Younger people and women reported the strongest effect.
Your turn: your cause wants people to speak up (share a story, sign a public letter, show their face). Name one group in your cause who would likely self-censor under watch, and one thing you could design so they can act without being exposed.
Compare with one sharp reading
A strong answer pairs a real group with a real design fix. Example: "Nurses reporting unsafe staffing fear their managers, so a public petition with names will get almost no signatures. A better design is a private, aggregated survey that reports numbers, not names. The point isn't to hide; it's to lower the cost of telling the truth."

3 · Whose safety are you spending?

Every media move for a cause exposes someone, or protects them. For each case, make two calls. First, how much does this expose a real person, low, medium, or high? Second, what's the right move? The obvious "post it, it'll spread" is often the wrong one, because it spends someone else's safety. Commit both, then Check.
1. You make a public explainer graphic for your issue: facts, numbers, and a call to action. No people appear in it.
Exposure
Move
Low exposure, so publish openly. Public is not automatically dangerous. When a message carries no person's name, face, or location, the fast, open feed is exactly the right tool. Save your caution for the cases that actually cost someone.
2. At a rally, a woman gives a raw, moving speech about her eviction. Filming and posting it would boost your reach, but she is afraid her landlord will retaliate.
Exposure
Move
High exposure, and her informed consent comes first, before a frame is posted. Ask plainly what she is comfortable with, and offer to blur or use only audio. Her reach is worth less than her home. If she says no, you have your answer.
3. You want to screenshot your private organizing group chat to show momentum and recruit. The screenshot includes members' names and profile photos.
Exposure
Move
Medium exposure. Members opted into a private room, not a public post, so the fix is to strip the identifying details: recreate the message clean, or crop and blur. You keep the proof of momentum and spend nobody's privacy to get it. This is the same move as blurring a face.
4. An anonymous insider emails you documents exposing wrongdoing by your cause's opponent. Posting them would help. Careless posting could reveal who leaked.
Exposure
Move
High exposure, to the source. Protect them and verify first. Documents carry hidden metadata that can name the leaker, and a false document can sink your cause. Strip the metadata, confirm the facts, and never expose who handed it over. This is the samizdat instinct: guard the person before the page.
5. For convenience, your volunteer contact list lives in a Google Sheet set to "anyone with the link can view."
Exposure
Move
Medium exposure, and rising. A "just a spreadsheet" list is exactly the single seizable point samizdat was built to avoid. One shared link, one search, and your whole network is mapped. Lock it to named people only. The politics of the low bridge apply to a permissions setting too.
6. A sympathetic local reporter wants the real names of your quietly organizing members for a feature story that could help your cause.
Exposure
Move
Medium exposure, and it is not yours to spend. Even a friendly story is public and permanent. Let each member decide for themselves; some will step forward and some will stay in the trusted core, and both are fine. Never hand over a name that its owner did not offer.

4 · A newsletter that outlived its own editors

Moscow, 1968–1983

The Chronicle of Current Events was a samizdat newsletter that did one plain, dangerous thing: it documented arrests, trials, and abuses, dryly and accurately, for about fifteen years. Editors were arrested; the Chronicle kept coming. Its secret was structural. There was no central subscriber list to seize and no single office to raid. Each issue told readers, in effect, "if you know of an arrest, pass the facts back up the same chain you got this from." Information traveled through trust, in both directions, and the network had no middle for the KGB to cut.

Notice the callback: the Chronicle's safety came from the same thing you felt Tuesday. The medium had no center. That is Winner's point, turned into a survival tactic, and it is the exact opposite of a public list on a shared link.

Your turn: the Chronicle was harder to kill than one big publication would have been, precisely because it was decentralized. Name one thing about your cause you would deliberately keep decentralized (no single list, no one account, no lone organizer), and why.
Compare with one sharp reading
"I would never let our whole contact network live in one person's phone. If that organizer burns out or that account gets locked, the cause shouldn't die with it. Spread across a few trusted people, it survives any single failure, the same reason the Chronicle outlived its editors."

Samizdat

From Russian sam (self) + izdat (publishing): the underground copying and hand-to-hand passing of censored texts in the USSR, roughly the 1950s through the 1980s. Readers retyped banned works and circulated them privately, since the State owned every legal press.

Example: a poem or an arrest report typed at a kitchen table, carbon-copied five or six sheets at a time, then handed to trusted friends who retyped it again. No publisher, no store, no list.

Carbon copy

Before photocopiers, you stacked thin sheets with sheets of inky carbon paper between them. Type or press hard on top, and the pressure prints the same marks onto every layer at once. That is where "cc" in email comes from.

Example: a samizdat typist could make up to about six copies in one typing. Hit the keys harder, get more copies, but the bottom sheets came out faint. That faintness is the "carbon-copy fade" you saw drift down Tuesday's chain.

The mountain ballad tradition

Long before recordings, songs and stories traveled the Southern Appalachians the same slow, human way samizdat did: person to person, sung or hand-copied into a family "ballad book" and passed along. Each carrier kept a song alive by teaching it to the next, and small changes crept in with every pass.

A little history: many of these ballads arrived with Scots-Irish and English settlers and were carried by memory through the mountains for generations, collected only much later. The remedy books and recipe cards handed down the same way follow the same logic.

Why it's in this course: it is a home-grown "reader is the publisher" network. No central press, high trust, and it held a culture together across generations by the simple act of passing it on. That is the social doing the work, the exact thing this course tries to put back.

Langdon Winner

Political theorist of technology. His 1980 essay "Do Artifacts Have Politics?" (Week 2) argues that the way we design tools and systems can quietly enforce power, no law required.

Here: a typewriter with carbon paper "has politics" too. Its limits forced samizdat to be decentralized, which is precisely what let it survive.

The chilling effect

When people believe they are being watched, they hold back from things they are legally free to do: they read less, say less, and avoid certain topics. The harm is the silence itself, produced without a single arrest.

Example: after the 2013 NSA surveillance revelations, searches for words like "car bomb" dropped on Wikipedia. Nobody was charged; people just stopped, because they felt watched.

Self-censorship

Editing or silencing yourself out of fear of consequences, before anyone tells you to. It is how surveillance mostly works: not by punishing speech, but by making people quietly decide it isn't worth the risk.

Example: a nurse who won't sign a public petition about unsafe staffing because her manager might see it. No rule stopped her. The watching did.

Exposure, low to high

Not a box, a slider, like Week 2's fast–slow scale. It asks: how much does this media move reveal about a real person, and how badly could that hurt them?

Low: facts and figures, no person named. Medium: a name or face of someone who chose some visibility but not this. High: reveals someone who could face retaliation, arrest, or real harm. The skill is reading the case, not defaulting to "post it."

Informed consent

A person agrees to be shown after they clearly understand what will happen: where it posts, how public it is, that it may be permanent, and that they can say no. A quick "is this okay?" in a loud moment is not it.

With vs. without: Without: you post the rally clip because she spoke at a public event. With: you show her the clip, explain it will go on a public page forever, offer audio-only, and post only if she says yes. Same footage, very different ethics.

Metadata

Data about data: the hidden information a file carries along with its contents. A document or photo can quietly store who made it, when, on what device, and even where.

Example: a leaked Word file can name its author in its properties, and a phone photo can hold GPS coordinates. Post it raw and you may expose the very person you meant to protect. Stripping metadata is a basic act of care.
