Tuesday, in one line: a hashtag can gather a huge crowd in days, but a crowd with no leaders, no agreed demand, and no way to decide together freezes the moment it's asked "what do you want, and who's in charge?" Zeynep Tufekci calls that the tactical freeze. Today we read why it happens, then build the muscle that prevents it.

1 · Scaling without the muscle

Older movements were forced to do slow, tedious organizing before they could gather anyone: the meetings, the phone trees, the training. Digital networks let you skip all of it and summon a crowd instantly. Tufekci's argument is that the slow work was secretly doing a second job. It was building the capacity to act as one. Skip the work, and you get the crowd without the capacity.

See it
Think of two ways to get 100,000 people into a square. Way one: six months of organizing, so by the time they arrive they already know each other, trust each other, and have agreed what they want. Way two: a hashtag that pulls 100,000 strangers overnight who share a feeling and nothing else. Same photo from a helicopter. Completely different machine underneath. The first can negotiate, pivot, and follow through. The second often can't, because there's no "underneath" at all.
Then and now: the purest fast-and-empty case
You have watched this happen. The flash mob, strangers summoned by a post to appear in a train station, dance for two minutes, and vanish, is Tufekci's argument in miniature. Instant assembly, zero capacity, nothing underneath, and by design it disperses before it could ever decide anything. A protest built the same way can pull a stunning crowd overnight and still have no way to turn that crowd into a demand anyone can answer.
Read the real thing (8–10 min)

Tufekci, Twitter and Tear Gas, the Introduction (free full book, Creative Commons PDF from the author's site). Read the Introduction, roughly the first 15 pages. She lays out the whole argument there.

"There is no need to spend six months putting together a single rally when a hashtag could be used to summon protesters into the streets… However, the tedious work performed during the pre-internet era served other purposes as well; perhaps most importantly, it acclimatized people to the processes of collective decision making."
Your turn: name one thing your cause could do this week that would gather attention fast, and one slower thing that would build capacity. Which one are you actually doing right now, and which are you skipping?
Compare with one sharp reading
A strong answer notices that the fast move produces a spike and the slow move produces a floor. Example: "FAST is a viral before/after photo of the polluted creek. SLOW is a text list of 40 neighbors who agreed to show up when asked. We have the photo instinct and no list, so every spike lands on nobody."

2 · Why leaderless freezes

Being leaderless sounds like pure democracy, and it has a real upside: no single person can be arrested, bought off, or discredited to kill the movement. Tufekci is careful and fair about that. The cost shows up later. With no agreed way to decide, the group can't choose a new tactic, can't authorize anyone to negotiate, and can't take a partial win. So it defaults to repeating the one move everyone already recognizes.

See it
In Egypt in 2011, occupying Tahrir Square toppled a president. When the moment called for a different move, an organization, a candidate, a negotiation, the crowd had no way to agree on one. Every road led back to the square. Tufekci's phrase for it: "Tahrir or bust." The thing that first gave them power, a giant leaderless occupation, was the only thing they could do, so it stopped working.
Read the real thing (8–10 min)

Tufekci, Twitter and Tear Gas, Chapter 3: "Leading the Leaderless" (same free PDF, jump to Chapter 3). Read the opening pages where she defines the freeze. Watch how careful she is to name the upside of leaderlessness before the cost.

"Often unable to change course after the initial, speedy expansion phase, they exhibit a 'tactical freeze.'… by design, by choice, and by the evolution of these movements, they lack… the mechanisms to develop and agree on new paths to take."
Your turn: if your cause got its biggest moment tomorrow and someone in power offered you half of what you want, who in your cause is allowed to say yes or no? If the answer is "nobody" or "everybody," you've found your freeze.
Compare with one sharp reading
A strong answer names the missing decision structure, beyond a vague "we're disorganized." Example: "No one is authorized to negotiate, and we've never agreed how we'd decide. So we'd freeze, restate the maximum demand, and lose the deal. The fix is boring: agree now on three people and a rule for how they decide."

3 · Capacity or freeze? Diagnose the strategy

Each case is a real strategy choice a cause could make. Two judgments, and the "impressive" answer is often the fragile one. First, does the move build durable capacity, or is it fast-but-fragile? Second, which strategy element is doing the work, or missing? Commit both, then Check.
1. A cause's reaction video hits 250,000 views overnight. They have no email list, no next post planned, and no way to reach those viewers again.
Leans
The element
Fragile, and the missing piece is a calendar / rhythm. A quarter million people met the cause once and will never hear from it again. The spike lands on nobody because there's no floor to catch it. "Impressive" numbers, zero capacity.
2. A cause posts one useful thing every Tuesday to 300 followers who have come to expect it. Nothing goes viral. The 300 open it every week.
Leans
The element
Durable, built by the editorial calendar. Small and unglamorous, but 300 people who reliably show up is a floor you can stand on. When the big moment comes, this cause already exists in people's week. The trade is reach for dependability.
3. A cause is on TikTok, LinkedIn, X, and a newsletter, posting the identical stiff press-release paragraph to all four.
Leans
The element
Mixed leaning fragile, and the missing piece is platform voice. Being everywhere looks like strength; the same press-release tone works nowhere. A platform-appropriate voice would say the same thing four ways. Presence without fit is just noise in four places.
4. A hashtag pulls 5,000 people to the courthouse in 48 hours. A reporter asks who speaks for them and what they want. Nobody can answer, and the offer of a meeting goes unanswered.
Leans
The element
Fragile, and the missing piece is a decision structure. This is the tactical freeze in its purest form: huge turnout, no one authorized to speak or commit, so the movement can't negotiate or pivot. The crowd is real; the muscle to use it is not there.
5. A cause defines its audience precisely (renters in one zip code who have gotten an eviction notice), learns what they already believe, and speaks only to them, on the one app they actually use. Reach is small on purpose.
Leans
The element
Durable, built on a defined audience. Small reach is the trade, and it's worth it. Speaking precisely to the people who can actually act beats broadcasting to strangers who can't. This is the opposite of the freeze: a narrow, real base that can move.
6. A cause has a gorgeous six-month editorial calendar, posts like clockwork, and grows steadily. Asked what all of it is for, the answer is "raising awareness."
Leans
The element
Mixed. The calendar is real capacity, so this isn't fragile in the reach sense. What's missing is a goal. "Awareness" isn't an aim you can win or lose, so the machine runs beautifully toward nothing. Motion is not the same as direction.

4 · A crowd that could act as one

Washington, D.C., 1963

The March on Washington looked like the country spontaneously showing up. It was the opposite. In about eight weeks, Bayard Rustin engineered more than 2,000 chartered buses and 21 special trains, a trained volunteer marshal corps, the sound system, the water, the parking, and 80,000 boxed lunches. A quarter of a million people arrived and left in a single day with almost no incident, because the machine underneath had been built first.

Tufekci's sharpest point is about what that machine signaled. A march that flawless proved a hidden capacity: "we can coordinate a quarter million people, so imagine what else we can coordinate." That threatened power. A hashtag can now produce a bigger crowd overnight without proving any such capacity, which is part of why the sheer size of a protest means less than it used to.

Stay honest and careful: the movements Tufekci studies faced real violence (Egypt's uprising was followed by a brutal crackdown; Gezi Park protesters were met with tear gas). The comparison here is structural, how a movement is built and what it can do, not a ranking of anyone's courage or suffering. Fragile capacity is a design problem, never a moral verdict on the people in the street.
Your turn: the 1963 march signaled capacity. What, really, does your cause's current activity signal to the people who could give you what you want? Strength you can back up, or a mood that will pass?
Compare with one sharp reading
"Right now our activity signals a mood: a few angry posts when something bad happens, then silence. To the county commission that reads as 'this will blow over.' If we signaled a standing list of 200 residents who show up every month, that reads as 'this will not blow over,' and that's the thing that actually moves them."

Flash mob

A group summoned by social media or text to gather suddenly in a public place, do one brief thing, and scatter, a pillow fight, a frozen "freeze," a two-minute dance in a food court.

Example: the mid-2000s train-station dance videos. Pure fast media: instant assembly, no organization, no lasting group, gone in minutes. A useful picture of a crowd with no capacity underneath it.

What a flash mob is →

Zeynep Tufekci

Sociologist who studies technology, movements, and society (she grew up in Turkey and lived through a military coup, which sharpened her eye for how power and media interact). Her 2017 book Twitter and Tear Gas is the definitive study of why networked protests rise so fast and stall so hard.

Example: she was on the ground for Tahrir Square and Gezi Park, and noticed the same pattern in both: enormous crowds, no way to decide what to do next.

About Zeynep Tufekci →

The tactical freeze

Tufekci's term for a movement's inability to change tactics, negotiate, or agree on a new demand after it has already scaled up fast. It grows out of being leaderless with no agreed way to decide.

Example: Occupy Wall Street could hold a park for months but could not agree on a single concrete demand, so when the moment passed, there was no next move to make. That is a freeze.

Capacity

A movement's built-up ability to actually do things together: make decisions, coordinate people, follow through, and change course. It's the machine underneath a crowd, not the crowd itself.

With / without: WITH capacity, 300 people can run a year-long boycott and negotiate its end. WITHOUT it, 300,000 people can fill a square and then dissolve because no one can decide what to do next.

Leaderless / horizontal

A movement with no designated leaders or official spokespeople, run by the whole group. Also called "horizontal" organizing.

The trade: the upside is real, no single leader can be arrested, bribed, or discredited to end the movement. The cost is that with no one authorized to decide, the group often can't negotiate or pivot. Strength and weakness in the same choice.

About horizontal organizing →

Tahrir Square / the Arab Spring

In 2011, a wave of uprisings swept the Arab world. In Egypt, mass occupations of Cairo's Tahrir Square forced out President Hosni Mubarak in 18 days.

Why it's here: the occupation was leaderless and internet-coordinated, which made it fast and hard to behead. Afterward, with no organization or agreed leadership, the movement couldn't convert the moment into lasting power, and the military eventually reasserted control. Tufekci's "Tahrir or bust."

About the 2011 Egyptian revolution →

Gezi Park protests

A 2013 uprising in Istanbul, Turkey, that began as a sit-in to stop a park from being paved over and grew into a nationwide protest against the government. Tufekci was there.

Why it's here: huge, leaderless, and organized on social media, it hit the same tactical freeze, and was met with heavy police violence and tear gas (the "tear gas" in her title).

About the Gezi Park protests →

The March on Washington (1963)

The August 1963 civil rights march where roughly 250,000 people gathered at the Lincoln Memorial and Dr. King gave the "I Have a Dream" speech.

A little history: it looked spontaneous but took months of painstaking logistics, and it helped push the Civil Rights Act of 1964 forward. The flawless coordination itself was the message: this movement can organize at scale.

Appalachian tie: much of the civil rights movement's organizing muscle was trained at the Highlander Folk School in the Tennessee mountains, where Rosa Parks studied months before her arrest and where "We Shall Overcome" was adapted into the movement's anthem. Slow capacity-building, built in Appalachia, stood underneath the big moment.

About the March on Washington →

Bayard Rustin

The organizer who built the 1963 March on Washington in about eight weeks. A brilliant strategist and an openly gay Black man, he was often pushed out of the spotlight because of who he was, and did the invisible work anyway.

Example: he ran the buses, trains, marshals, sound, water, and 80,000 lunches from an office on index cards and phones. The "underneath" of the most famous protest in U.S. history was one person's logistics.

Signaling capacity

Tufekci's idea that a protest's real power is often what it proves to those in charge, not the event itself. A perfectly run march signals "we can coordinate at scale," which implies "we could also run a boycott, an election, or a strike."

Example: a flawless 250,000-person march worries a government more than a chaotic million-person one, because the flawless one advertises a machine that can be pointed at other targets.

Durable capacity

A move that leaves the cause stronger after the attention fades: a list of committed people, a habit, a clear demand, a way to decide. It builds a floor.

Example: converting 40 event attendees into a 40-person text list is durable. The event ends; the list stays and can be called on again.

Fast-but-fragile

A move that spikes attention but leaves nothing behind once it fades: a viral post with no follow-up, a crowd with no way to reconvene.

Example: a video with 500,000 views and no email list is fragile. Enormous reach, no relationship, no way to reach any of those people again.

Audience (in strategy)

Not "everyone," but the specific, named group whose action would actually move your cause, defined tightly enough that you can picture one real person in it.

With / without: WITHOUT: "people who care about the environment." WITH: "parents at Hardin Park Elementary who drop off on the busy road every morning." Only the second one tells you what to say and where to say it.

Goal (a real one)

An outcome you can actually win or lose and know when you've reached, not a vague mood like "awareness."

With / without: WITHOUT: "raise awareness about the crosswalk." WITH: "get 150 signatures to the town council before the November meeting." You can tell if the second one worked.

Editorial calendar

A simple plan for what you'll publish and when, so a cause keeps a steady rhythm instead of only posting when something dramatic happens. It's the "floor" that keeps you present between big moments.

Example: "every Wednesday: one short update; first of the month: one story from a member; whenever someone signs: a quick photo." It can live in a single spreadsheet.

What an editorial calendar is →

Platform-appropriate voice

Saying the same true thing in the register each platform and audience expects, instead of pasting one tone everywhere. The message holds; the delivery changes.

With / without: the same fact, "our creek failed its pollution test." On a TikTok: a 15-second walk to the water with the result on screen. In a grant email: "Recent testing showed levels exceeding the state threshold." Same truth, different clothes.

Jenny Odell, "How to Do Nothing"

Artist and writer whose 2017 talk (later a book) argues for reclaiming attention from platforms designed to capture it. Not literal idleness, but refusing to treat every minute as something to monetize or post.

Example: she grounds the talk in a rose garden in Oakland, using a public, unproductive place as a model for the attention the feed keeps stealing.

Read the free transcript →

Communicative capitalism

Jodi Dean's term (next week's idea) for a system where posting, sharing, and reacting feel like political action while mostly just feeding circulation. The message becomes one more contribution to the stream, decoupled from any real response.

Example: 100,000 shares of an outrage post can leave a cause exactly where it started, because the sharing was the whole event. We'll test your cause against this next week.
