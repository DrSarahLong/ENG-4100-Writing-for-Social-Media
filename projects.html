Major Projects

Three projects carry the course, and each one builds on the last. You analyze how a movement works, build a narrative strategy for your own cause, then make and measure a campaign that exists in the world. Each project is scored on a handful of criteria, and the weekly modules walk you through building it.

How to read a rubric. Each criterion is scored on one of four levels. Meets is the target: solid, full-credit work. Read the Meets column first, that's what you're aiming for on every line. Each level shows its points. A project's criteria split its weight evenly and add up to the whole (Project 1 totals 20, Project 2 totals 25, Project 3 totals 30), so you can see exactly what each line is worth.

Movement Media Analysis

Research-grounded analysis · submitted as a Google Doc

Pick one movement, one of our cases or one you bring me and I approve, and take apart how it actually reached people. Every movement runs on a media theory: a working set of beliefs about how ideas travel and how strangers become a "we." Your job is to name that theory, show its slow tools and its fast ones, read a few primary artifacts up close (a pamphlet, a poster, a chant, a broadcast, a hashtag), weigh the ethics of how it persuaded, then carry concrete lessons back to your own cause. Think close reading of how the reaching happened, backed by credible sources, about 1,500 to 2,000 words. You're explaining the mechanics of connection, not retelling the movement's history.

Need a movement? A few ideas beyond our cases

All have rich, researchable media. Pick one and clear it with me first:

  • Montgomery Bus Boycott: mimeographed handbills, church networks, carpool lists
  • United Farm Workers grape strike: El Malcriado, the huelga eagle, the boycott
  • Standing Rock / #NoDAPL: livestreams and a mass Facebook check-in
  • School climate strikes (Fridays for Future): Instagram and the hand-lettered sign
  • Disability rights, the 504 sit-ins & Capitol Crawl: press stunts and posters
  • AIDS Memorial Quilt: the quilt itself as circulating media
  • Occupy Wall Street: the "We Are the 99%" Tumblr and the human mic
  • #MeToo: the hashtag and testimony threads
  • Arab Spring (Egypt, 2011): the "We Are All Khaled Said" Facebook page
  • Hong Kong, 2019: Telegram channels, LIHKG, Lennon Walls
  • "Woman, Life, Freedom" (Iran, 2022): hashtags and phone video
  • Temperance / WCTU: pamphlets, ribbons, the pledge card (a good slow-media contrast)

Even a flame-out like Kony 2012 or the Ice Bucket Challenge works, if you analyze why the reach didn't last.

What it needs

  • About 1,500–2,000 words. One movement, read closely, not a survey of ten.
  • The movement's media theory, named and put to work (ritual vs. transmission, slow vs. fast, owning vs. renting the channel).
  • Three to five primary artifacts, quoted or described and read up close, plus credible secondary sources, cited consistently. A primary artifact is a piece of the movement's own communication: a newspaper page, a poster or flyer, a pamphlet or zine, a speech, a staged photograph, a protest sign or banner, a slogan or song, a manifesto or communiqué, a logo, or a specific hashtag, post, or thread. (A Wikipedia page or a news story about the movement is a secondary source, not an artifact.)
  • A clear read of how the media built community and moved ideas, with its tensions and trade-offs.
  • Concrete, usable lessons for your own cause, tied to what the case actually shows.

Models to study. Read one or two of these for how a strong analysis thinks, the depth of the reading and the way every claim is backed by evidence. Study the thinking, not the layout: a magazine piece, a research report, and a museum essay all look different, and none is the template for how yours should look on the page.

  • The Black Panther newspaper (Columbia Journalism Review): one movement's print paper read as its organizing engine.
  • Emory Douglas & the Panthers' visual identity (Hyperallergic): a movement's design and imagery as media.
  • SILENCE=DEATH and ACT UP (Smithsonian): one iconic graphic and a movement's design activism.
  • #BlackLivesMatter on Twitter (Pew Research): how a hashtag built reach and community, read through data.
  • How suffragists used spectacle and the press (History.com): parades, pageantry, and publicity as strategy.

Format & citations. Keep the document itself simple: basic essay style, a title, an intro, body paragraphs, a conclusion, your name and the date. No special layout required. Use whatever citation style you're comfortable with, as long as you're consistent. I find Chicago easiest to read for this kind of analysis (footnotes keep your close readings uninterrupted), but MLA or APA are fine.

Narrative Strategy

Strategy document (roughly 2,000 words) + one completed Story Canvas · submitted as a Google Doc

Every cause is already inside a story somebody else wrote. Before your cause can win anything, that story has to change. So this project is not a posting schedule. You diagnose the dominant story about your cause, find the place where it can be broken, and then write the story you want people carrying around instead. You will use three named frameworks from movement communications, apply them to your own cause, and hand in a plan a volunteer could pick up and tell.

Read a completed example (PDF) A full strategy document written to this assignment, "Ready from Bell to Bell," on menstrual-supply access in Watauga County schools. Use it to see what the six steps look like finished, not as a template to copy.

The six steps

  1. Diagnose the story you are up against. Use the five elements of narrative from story-based strategy (below) to take apart the version of your cause that is already circulating. Not what you wish people thought. What they already think.
  2. Name the underlying assumption, and pick your point of intervention. Every story rests on something unstated that the audience has to accept for it to work. Find it. That assumption is the softest place to hit.
  3. Refuse the inherited frame and build your own. George Lakoff, a cognitive linguist, argued in Don't Think of an Elephant! (2004) that "activating a frame, even when rejecting it, strengthens the frame." So you cannot argue your way out of a frame. You have to speak from a different one.
  4. Write the public narrative. Marshall Ganz, an organizer who worked with the United Farm Workers and now teaches at Harvard, built a three-part structure movement leaders use: Story of Self, Story of Us, Story of Now. Each part is a challenge, a choice, and an outcome.
  5. Name your audience by what they value, not by their age. Then write one call to action that a person could complete this week.
  6. Fill out the Story Canvas and set your indicators. One page, eleven boxes, and a clear answer to "how would I know the story moved?"

Creative Campaign + Æffect Report

Three to five finished artifacts + an æffect report (roughly 1,500 words) · presented to the class

Take the narrative you built in Project 2 and make it exist in the world. Design, produce, and present a small, ethical campaign for your cause, then measure what it did to people and what it changed. The whole semester has been about putting the social back into social media, so here is the constraint that makes you do it: at least one of your artifacts cannot be distributable on social media. It has to need a body in a place. You may still build social pieces, and you may still run a social campaign if that is where your audience actually is, but you have to defend that choice rather than default to it.

What it needs

  • Three to five finished artifacts that clearly belong to one campaign, across more than one mode.
  • At least one artifact that cannot be distributed on social media, plus one paragraph on what being present adds that a post could not.
  • The narrative you built in Project 2, visibly carried into the artifacts: the same frame, the same characters, the same ask.
  • A named course theory driving the design choices, and a defense of any social channel you use.
  • Ethics and access designed in: disclosure, fair representation, digital accessibility where it applies, and at least three of the analog access moves above.
  • An æffect report (about 1,500 words) that walks all seven elements, reports affect and effect, and names what did not work.
  • Documentation of the artifacts, since the physical ones will not survive the semester: photographs, the object itself, or a scan.

The long version lives on its own page. What counts as an artifact that cannot travel online, the evidence for going creative, all seven æffect elements with an example of each, and how access works for handbuilt work. Open Project 3 details →

Field Notebook

A private Google Doc · turned in twice

Your working notebook holds the in-class exercises, your analog experiments (an undistracted conversation, a note circulated by hand, a day off the feed, a face-to-face ask), case notes, platform observations, and strategy scratch. It's graded on completion, not polish: full credit for doing the thinking and the fieldwork. You turn it in at the midterm check-in and again at the end of term.

Participation & Final Presentation

In-class engagement + a campaign presentation

Ten percent covers showing up ready and joining the class conversation all semester, plus your final presentation of the P3 campaign and impact report. A strong presentation tells the cause's story, shows the actual work, and reports the results with candor, including what didn't land.
