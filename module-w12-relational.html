1 · Craftivism

Craftivism is activism that makes something by hand and puts it in front of people who have to be somewhere to receive it. A stitched panel, a zine folded and handed over, chalk marks down a sidewalk showing how far it actually is to the nearest grocery store, a booth with two chairs in it.

Project 3 requires at least one artifact that cannot be distributed on social media. That constraint is the course's argument turned into an assignment: a thing that needs a body in a place does something a post cannot, and your job is to find out what.

The artifact and the record of it are two deliverables
Photograph everything you make, from the first attempt onward, and post whatever you want to post. You will need those photographs for the report, and you should bring the object itself to your presentation if it is something you can carry. The constraint is about where the artifact lives while the campaign is running, not about whether you are allowed to document it.
This is Project 3's setup. Project 3 is the Creative Campaign plus Æffect Report, 30% of the course, due Week 16 and presented in Weeks 15 and 16. You need at least two artifacts, at least one of which cannot be distributed on social media, and a report that walks all seven æffect elements. Everything you write today feeds it, so do it in your notebook rather than on scratch paper. 2 · The evidence for going creative

In 2018 the Center for Artistic Activism, with Silas Harrebye of Roskilde University and Stephen Duncombe of NYU, spent three days on a busy bridge in Copenhagen running conventional tactics against creative ones on the same issue: a speech against a performance, a petition table against something stranger. Across 108 spot interviews the creative versions outperformed the conventional ones on nearly every quantitative measure, and people described the conventional tactics as annoying and predictable while calling the creative ones funny and surprising. One bridge, one city, three days, so it is a strong result rather than a law. The full account is on the Project 3 details page.

3 · Six campaigns and the mechanism each one used

You are about to generate a lot of ideas, and the raw material for that is other people's mechanisms. So read these six first, and pay attention to how each one works rather than what it was about. Then match each campaign to the mechanism doing the work. Commit to an answer before you check; the point is the sorting, not the score.

1987 · San Francisco, then Washington

The AIDS Memorial Quilt

Cleve Jones, an activist frustrated that the men dying around him were being recorded only as numbers, asked people to stitch a panel for someone they had lost. The NAMES Project first laid it out on the National Mall in October 1987 with 1,920 panels. By 1996 it held around 40,000 and was the last time it was displayed whole.

2015 · United Kingdom

Sarah Corbett's “Don't Blow It” handkerchiefs

Corbett, a former professional campaigner who burned out on confrontational tactics, founded the Craftivist Collective in 2009. For a living-wage campaign aimed at Marks & Spencer, volunteers hand-embroidered bespoke handkerchiefs, each one addressed to a specific board member, each carrying a message and taking hours to make. They were delivered as gifts, not demands. M&S announced a living-wage commitment the following year.

1974 onward · Chile, under Pinochet

The arpilleras

After the September 1973 coup, women whose husbands and sons had been taken by the regime met in church workshops and stitched scenes of what had happened to them onto burlap: soldiers, empty houses, the question ¿Dónde están? They were sold abroad through the Vicariate of Solidarity as folk craft, which is how they left the country at all while the press was censored and the families were being told nothing.

2017 · United States

Welcome Blanket

Artist Jayna Zweiman, responding to the proposed border wall, asked knitters and quilters to produce blankets using a combined length of yarn equal to the roughly 2,000 miles of the border. Each finished blanket came with a welcome note and was given to a person newly arrived in the country. The project was first shown at the Smart Museum of Art in Chicago.

2017 · United States

The Pussyhat Project

Krista Suh and Jayna Zweiman published a free knitting pattern weeks before the January 2017 Women's March, and asked people to make hats for marchers and for themselves. Tens of thousands wore them. The project was also criticized, at the time and since, for a design and a name that did not represent trans women or women of color.

2018 onward · United States

Tiny Pricks Project

Diana Weymar began embroidering statements by President Trump onto vintage domestic textiles, doilies and linens and half-finished needlework, and invited others to do the same. Thousands of pieces came in. A sentence produced instantly on a phone took hours to render in thread on a cloth somebody's grandmother had started.

What to take from this
Six mechanisms, and not one of them is “post about it.” Any of the six will fit a cause it was never built for, so when you spec your own artifact below, check which of these six it is running on. If the answer is none of them, that is worth knowing before you buy materials.

4 · Give your idea dimensions

An artifact must have six things to exist: substance, size, quantity, location, longevity and the verb (meaning, what a person actually does with it).

Below are six artifact descriptions. Each one has five of the six and is missing exactly one. Pick which one is absent.

1 · A card table in the atrium

Two hundred 4×6 cards, cream stock, one rubber stamp in black, out on a table in the student union atrium for three weeks.

2 · A banner at the crossing

A hand-lettered banner on a canvas drop cloth, two colors, propped at the Rivers Street crossing on Tuesday and Thursday mornings for two weeks, read by people waiting to cross.

3 · Comment cards in the atrium

Hand-stamped index cards, 4×6, cream stock, on a table in the atrium for three weeks, and each person writes one line on the back and drops it in the slot.

4 · A zine, handed over

A one-sheet zine, eight panels, 140 copies, folded by hand, with the ask on panel two, handed to people over three weeks.

5 · A quilt in the library lobby

A quilt of 83 squares, six inches each, cotton scraps, every square stitched by a different person, assembled and hung in the library lobby.

6 · A sign at the crossing

One sign, 24 by 36 inches, hand-lettered in two colors, propped at the crossing, up for two weeks, read by people waiting.

Your turn: take the candidate you liked best on Tuesday and write it out with all six answered.
Compare with one strong reading
A strong answer reads like something you could hand to a stranger with twenty dollars and get the right object back. "SUBSTANCE: scrap plywood from the Maker Space offcut bin, exterior house paint in two colors, hand-lettered. SIZE: 24 by 36, because that is the biggest piece in the bin and it is readable from the far lane. QUANTITY: one, because the point is that a person obviously made it. LOCATION: propped at the Rivers Street crossing, east side, 8 to 9 a.m. LONGEVITY: Tuesdays and Thursdays for three weeks, carried in and out each time so it does not get removed. VERB: people read it while they wait, and the bottom third is a QR code to the two-line ask." A weak answer still says "a sign about the crossing."

5 · Level up: six things the strongest actions do

The Center for Artistic Activism has run this work with thousands of organizers. These six show up over and over in the actions that land. Use them as a revision checklist on the artifact you just spec’d.

Center celebration, hope, and joy. Grief and anger bring people once. Joy brings them back, and it is rarer in activism than it should be.
Use mystery, whimsy, and surprise. If someone can instantly file your work as “a political thing,” they dismiss it without thinking. Surprise buys you a few seconds where they have to work out what they are looking at, and that is the only window you get.
Create irresistible imagery. Set aside the picture in your head of what a protest looks like. What is a large, strange, visual way to take up space? What is your message as an image rather than a sentence?
Make it personal. Nobody wants to be one unit of someone else's victory. One-on-one, tailored, specific.
Pursue risk and innovation. Not danger. The risk of doing something that might not work, which is the only way anything new happens.
Invite meaningful participation. The person does something rather than watching something. This one does the most for Project 3, because an artifact people act on is also an artifact that measures itself.
Your turn: pick your strongest surviving idea from Tuesday and say which two of the six it already does. Then pick one it fails at and change the idea so it does that one too.
Compare with one strong reading
A strong answer changes the object rather than adding a word to the description. "It does IMAGERY and PARTICIPATION. It fails at JOY, because every line on it is about something bad that happened. Change: half the cards ask what it would feel like if the problem were fixed, so the wall has both halves and people leave on the second one."

6 · Work the Æffect App

Before you spend money or book a machine, put the idea through the planning tool the Center for Artistic Activism built for exactly this. The Æffect App walks the same seven elements your Project 3 report has to walk, and it asks the questions in the order that stops you from building the wrong thing.

The seven stages, in the app's own words
1. Goals: what do you want to accomplish? · 2. Audience: who do you want to reach? · 3. Ethics: what principles guide your work? · 4. Intervention: what do you want to create? · 5. Metrics: how will you know if it works? · 6. Evaluation: did it work? · 7. Iteration: what can you improve for next time?
How to run it today. It is free, it is from the same people as the toolkit, and it is licensed CC BY-NC-SA. It does require you to make an account with an email and a password, so know that going in. Then:

1. Register, then create a project and give it your campaign's name.
2. Work through stages 1 through 5. Those you can answer now.
3. Leave 6 and 7 alone. Evaluation and Iteration cannot be answered until the campaign has run, and the app will still be there in Week 14 when you have results to put in them.
4. Open the Report page. Select all of it, copy it, and paste it into your Field Notebook in the box below. (The report page is built to print, so printing to PDF works too.)
Why this before the materials list
Stage 5 is the one that reorders your thinking. “How will you know if it works?” asked before you build will often change what you build, because an artifact nobody can count is an artifact you cannot report on in Week 14. If answering stage 5 makes you want to change stage 4, that is the app working.
The complication: the app will ask you things you cannot answer yet, and the temptation is to invent an answer so the box is full. Write “not decided yet” instead, and note what you would have to find out. A plan with three blanks in it is more useful than one with three guesses, and the blanks are what you take to the Maker Space.

Craftivism

Activism that works through making things by hand, usually slowly, usually in a way that asks the maker and the receiver to be present. The term was coined by Betsy Greer in 2003; Sarah Corbett's Craftivist Collective later built a method around what she calls “gentle protest.”

Example: the AIDS Memorial Quilt, which you read in Week 8. Each panel is three by six feet, the size of a grave, stitched by someone who lost a person. Alone it is a blanket. Assembled it covered the National Mall, and the act of walking between the panels is the argument.

Why it is here: a handmade object carries evidence of the time somebody spent on it. That is information a post cannot transmit.

The NAMES Project

The organization Cleve Jones and others founded in San Francisco in 1987 to collect and display the AIDS Memorial Quilt. That title is the argument. The US government was publishing case counts, and nobody was recording who the dead actually were.

Example of the mechanism: each panel is three feet by six feet because that is roughly the footprint of a grave. Walking the display means walking a cemetery that was never allowed to exist.

The Quilt's own history page ↗

The Craftivist Collective

Founded in London in 2009 by Sarah Corbett, a professional campaigner who had worked in conventional activism since she was a teenager and burned out on it. Her method, which she calls gentle protest, is slow, handmade, and aimed at one person at a time rather than at a crowd.

Her reasoning, and you can argue with it: an aggressive tactic can win the room and lose the person who has to actually change the policy. A critic would say gentleness lets power off easy. Both positions are defensible, and your Project 3 takes a side whether you say so or not.

Arpillera

Spanish for burlap or sackcloth, and the word for the Chilean patchwork pictures stitched from scrap fabric onto a burlap backing. After the 1973 coup that installed Augusto Pinochet, women in Santiago church workshops used them to record disappearances the press could not report.

Example: a typical panel shows small figures outside a prison wall, or a kitchen with one chair empty, often with ¿Dónde están? (“Where are they?”) stitched across it. Sold abroad as craft souvenirs, they carried the testimony out of the country.

The Appalachian tie: quilting and needlework here have the same double life. A quilt is a domestic object nobody inspects, which is why it has repeatedly been the thing women made when they had something to say and no platform to say it on.
