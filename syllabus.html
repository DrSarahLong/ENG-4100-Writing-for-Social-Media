Course description

This section of ENG 4100 focuses on writing for digital and social media, with emphasis on ethics, audience, circulation, and social impact. We study how ideas travel, how a message finds an audience, and how writers compose responsibly for public, networked spaces. You will analyze real movements, build a content strategy, and produce and measure a small multimodal campaign of your own, working in platform-appropriate voices and thinking hard about the effects your writing has on real people.

The frame: every movement runs on a media theory. Long before the smartphone, abolitionists, suffragists, and underground publishers built connection with the media they had: pamphlets, newspapers, banners, hand-copied pages, and face-to-face conversation. Each made a choice about how to reach people and draw them together. We study those choices, slow and fast, and use them to put the "social" back into social media, treating connection as a real skill you can build rather than a metric you chase. Tuesdays are the analog case; Thursdays carry it to today's platforms.

Course objectives

You will practice: rhetorical analysis of a movement's media; composing for specific platforms, audiences, and purposes; the ethics of digital writing (disclosure, privacy, representation, accessibility, and algorithmic bias); building a content strategy, audit, and editorial calendar; producing multimodal texts that combine language, image, sound, and design; and measuring circulation and real impact rather than vanity metrics.

How this course works

We meet twice a week, in person, at 11:00 in Sanford Hall, Room 504. Tuesdays are analog: a historical movement and a live, hands-on connection or observation exercise, met in the room. Thursdays are digital: a self-contained interactive module that takes Tuesday's idea to contemporary platforms. From Week 3 on you work through the Thursday module during our class time while I hold the Zoom Room open for orientation and questions; attendance is still expected. Weeks 1 and 2 meet on campus both days.

Your cause runs through everything. In Week 1 you choose one real cause you genuinely care about, ideally one that hasn't gained much traction yet, so your media work actually matters. You carry it through every case study, your Field Notebook, and all three projects. We pressure-test your pick together in Week 1 and lock it by the end of the week.

Your Field Notebook (15%)

Your working notebook is a Google Sheet you copy once and keep in your own Drive. It holds your in-class exercises, analog experiments (a real undistracted conversation, a note you circulate by hand, a day off the feed, a face-to-face ask), case notes, platform observations, and strategy scratch. It's lighter than an essay journal, more observation and documentation. It's graded on completion, not polish, and collected twice, at the midterm check-in and at the end of term, when you share it with me. It stays private in your own account until you submit.

Teaching approach

This class runs like a workshop, not a lecture. There are no slide decks. I teach from a live worked example and guided, scaffolded practice, and you learn by doing the analysis and the making yourself. Much of the Tuesday work is observational and personal, so your Field Notebook stays private on your own account; you submit it twice for a completion grade, and it is never shared with your classmates. You set the pace within each week, you choose the cause you care about, and I'll support anyone who takes the work seriously.

Policies

The work you turn in is your own
Your analysis, strategy, and campaign should be yours. Use of outside sources is welcome and expected, especially in Projects 1 and 2, and those sources must be cited. Any images, audio, or other media you use in a campaign must be yours, properly licensed, or fairly used and credited.
Deadlines
The three projects are due on the dates posted on the Schedule and in Canvas (roughly Week 6, Week 11, and Week 16). The Field Notebook is collected twice, at the midterm check-in and at the end of term. Presentations run in the last two weeks.
Late work
Late project work is not accepted except under extraordinary, documented circumstances. Talk to me early, not after.
Attendance & participation
We meet twice a week and attendance is expected both days, including the Thursday module sessions on Zoom. Much of the learning happens live in the room and in the class conversation, so being here, ready, is part of your grade.
The privacy of your writing
Your Field Notebook lives privately in your own Google account. You share it with me only at the midterm and end-of-term collections, for a completion grade. You never have to share anything you're not ready to.
AI / ChatGPT
Using AI is not plagiarism in this class, but I expect you to use it appropriately and ethically. See these guidelines for the ethical use of generative AI (Santa Clara University). Learning to use it well is part of the skill set, but this course is about developing your voice, so lean on it thoughtfully.

Required materials

Everything for this course is free and open-access. Nothing to buy.

  • Digital Rhetoric: Theory, Method, Practice by Douglas Eyman (University of Michigan Press, 2015), free to read online in the open-access edition. Selected chapters, assigned by week.
  • Bad Ideas About Writing (West Virginia University Libraries, 2017), a free open textbook. Selected short chapters.
  • Twitter and Tear Gas by Zeynep Tufekci, the full book free online. Used in the networked-protest unit.
  • Short, free, open-access readings, essays, and primary sources, linked directly in each week's module.
  • A Google account for your Field Notebook (a Google Sheet you copy once) and a computer or tablet you can write on.
