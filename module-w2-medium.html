Week 2 · Thursday module

The Medium Is Not Neutral

Tuesday you felt it. Today you build the theory, stress-test it, and aim it at your own cause.
This module is your Thursday class. Move at your own pace, but do the whole thing, the reading, the diagnosis, the case, and the writing are the instruction, not extra credit. Keep your Field Notebook open and your cause in mind.
Tuesday, in one line: the same four words ("I need your help") meant five different things depending on how we sent them. The medium wasn't a neutral pipe. Today we name two theories behind that, push on where they break, and put them to work.

1 · "The medium is the message"

In 1964, before anyone had a feed, Marshall McLuhan made a claim that still unsettles: the real effect of any medium isn't the content it carries. It's how the medium reorganizes how we perceive, relate, and pay attention.

See it
A light bulb carries no "content," no words, no images. Yet it reshaped human life: night became usable, so work, cities, and nightlife changed. The bulb's message was the new world it made possible. Now swap in the feed. Its real message isn't any single post. It's that it trained a generation to think in fast, scattered fragments where print once trained people to think in long, private lines.
Read the real thing (5–8 min)

McLuhan, "The Medium is the Message" (MIT, free PDF). Read the first three or four pages. It reads like poetry, not a manual, so read it for the turns of phrase.

"The medium is the message. This is merely to say that the personal and social consequences of any medium result from the new scale that is introduced into our affairs by each extension of ourselves."
Your turn: pick one platform you use daily. What "new scale" does it introduce, what does it make bigger, faster, or more constant than human life used to be?
Compare with one sharp reading
A strong answer names the scale, not the app. Example: "TikTok introduces the scale of the infinite, there is never a last video, so 'enough' stops being a category. McLuhan would say the endlessness is the message, more than any clip in it."

2 · "Do artifacts have politics?"

Tuesday's bridge came from Langdon Winner, who asked a strange question in 1980: can a physical object have politics? His answer: yes. Design choices can enforce power quietly, with no law and no sign.

See it
Robert Moses' low parkway bridges on Long Island were built too low for buses. Cars (wealthier, whiter) passed; buses (poorer riders, disproportionately Black families) could not reach the beach. The exclusion was poured into the concrete. You can repeal a law. You cannot vote against a bridge.
Stay honest: historians argue about the bus detail (other rules also barred buses, and Moses' exact intent is contested, see Bernward Joerges' rebuttal). The clean example nobody disputes is hostile architecture: a park bench with a bar down the middle so no one can lie down. A whole politics ("no unhoused people here") built into a bench. Winner's principle survives the messy example.

Now your phone. The algorithm is the new low bridge. It decides who is even allowed to see you, it rewards outrage over nuance, and it optimizes for engagement rather than truth or need. Your cause will be shaped, and sometimes fought, by politics built into the platform before you post a word.

Read the real thing (6–8 min)

Winner, "Do Artifacts Have Politics?" (Monoskop, free PDF). Read the opening and the bridge passage. Notice he never says technology is evil, only that it is never neutral.

Your turn: name one design choice on a platform your cause uses that quietly includes some people and excludes others. Who is the "bus" that can't get through?
Compare with one sharp reading
A strong answer finds the excluded party, the person the design shuts out, rather than a mere annoyance. Example: "Reels reward polish and youth. Our cause is led by older tenants who don't want their faces online. The medium's 'politics' is that it makes the most affected people the least visible spokespeople."

3 · Diagnose the medium

Two judgments per case, and the honest answer is often "mixed." First, where does it fall on the fast–slow spectrum? Second, what is it really trading? Commit both, then Check.
1. An organizer posts a raw three-minute video that unexpectedly hits 500,000 views.
Leans
Trade
Fast, trading depth for reach. Half a million saw it; almost none will remember it next week, and you have no way to reach them again. Reach without a relationship.
2. You launch a paid newsletter; 90 people subscribe and read your long essays every week.
Leans
Trade
Mixed. The tech is fast and scalable, but long essays and paying, committed readers make its function slow. It trades reach for depth. Ninety real readers can outrun 500,000 strangers.
3. You run a private, invite-only Discord for 40 core volunteers.
Leans
Trade
Mixed. Instant, digital, always-on (fast), yet gated and intimate (slow). Its main trade is safety and control for public reach, nobody finds you by accident, which is the point.
4. You mail a printed quarterly zine to 200 dues-paying members.
Leans
Trade
Slow, trading reach for depth and trust. Expensive and small, but a physical object in someone's hands four times a year builds loyalty a feed can't.
5. You buy targeted ads shown to 100,000 strangers in your county.
Leans
Trade
Fast, trading depth for reach. Huge, instant, and paid, so it stops the moment the money does. Rented attention, not earned relationship.
6. You host a monthly potluck where people actually meet face to face.
Leans
Trade
Slow, trading reach for depth and trust. Twelve people in a room is the strongest medium there is, and the hardest to scale. This is where the "social" actually lives.

4 · A case with almost no "fast" in it

Montgomery, 1955

The Montgomery Bus Boycott ran for 381 days and had no internet, no TV strategy, barely any "fast" media at all. The night Rosa Parks was arrested, Jo Ann Robinson and the Women's Political Council secretly mimeographed roughly 35,000 handbills and had them distributed across Black Montgomery by morning. The rest ran on the slowest media there is: Sunday church announcements, carpool networks, and word passed person to person, week after week, for over a year.

Notice the callback: the boycott was about buses, the same buses Winner's bridges kept off the parkway. The medium and the message were the same object.

Your turn: the boycott had almost no reach tools and won anyway. What did all that slow media give the movement that a viral post never could? Name the thing in one sentence.
Compare with one sharp reading
"It gave them a network dense enough to hold a year-long sacrifice, 35,000 people who knew each other's faces will keep walking; 500,000 strangers who liked a post will not." Slow media builds the trust that makes hard, sustained action possible.

Medium

The channel or form that carries a message: a letter, a megaphone, a printed page, an app. The claim this week is that a medium is never just a neutral container. It shapes what the message can be.

Marshall McLuhan

Canadian media theorist (1911–1980). Coined "the medium is the message" and "the global village" in the 1960s, decades before the internet made him sound like a prophet.

Langdon Winner

Political theorist of technology. His 1980 essay "Do Artifacts Have Politics?" argues that the way we design tools and spaces can quietly enforce power, no law required.

The low bridges

Robert Moses shaped much of modern New York. On his Long Island parkways, overpasses were built too low for buses, which had the effect of keeping poorer, disproportionately Black bus riders away from the beaches. Design as gatekeeping.

Hostile architecture

Design meant to control behavior: benches you can't lie on, spikes under overpasses, armrests placed to prevent sleeping. The politics ("you're not welcome here") are built into the object.

The algorithm

The ranking system that decides which posts you see and in what order. It isn't neutral: it's tuned to maximize engagement, which tends to reward outrage, novelty, and frequency, and to bury anything that doesn't feed it. Your cause competes inside those rules.

The fast–slow spectrum

Not a box, a slider. Most real media sit somewhere in the middle and mix speeds. The skill isn't labeling; it's seeing what any point on the slider gains and gives up.

Montgomery Bus Boycott

1955–56 protest in Alabama against segregated buses, sparked by Rosa Parks' arrest and sustained for 381 days. A landmark of the civil rights movement, and a masterclass in slow media.

Jo Ann Robinson

English professor and head of the Women's Political Council in Montgomery. The night of Parks' arrest, she and a few students mimeographed tens of thousands of leaflets overnight, arguably the single most important act of "media production" in the boycott.

Zine

A small, self-made, cheaply-photocopied booklet (say "zeen," short for magazine), stapled together and passed hand to hand. A classic slow medium: tiny reach, high trust, total creative control.

Example: the Riot Grrrl punk-feminist zines of the early '90s spread a whole movement through the mail before it ever hit the news. You'll meet zines again in the ACT UP and Black Panther weeks.

Newsletter

A message (usually email) sent on a schedule to people who chose to subscribe. Unlike a feed, it lands directly in someone's inbox, and you own the list instead of renting an audience from an algorithm.

Example: platforms like Substack let a writer send long essays to paying subscribers. Ninety committed readers can matter more than a viral post.

About Substack →

Discord

A chat app built around private "servers," group spaces split into topic channels for text, voice, and video. Started with gamers; now runs everything from study groups to organizing hubs.

Example: a mutual-aid group might keep a members-only Discord where volunteers coordinate in real time, invisible to the public and the algorithm.

What Discord is →

Targeted ad

A paid post shown only to people who match traits a platform has inferred about you (age, location, interests, behavior). You pay to reach strangers who fit a profile.

Example: boosting a post to "women 25–40 within 20 miles who follow gardening pages." Powerful reach, but the attention stops the second the money does, and it raises real privacy questions.

Mimeograph

A hand-cranked copying machine (the "ditto") that pushed ink through a stencil, the cheap mass-printing of the mid-1900s, before photocopiers. Purple ink, a distinct smell, a few thousand copies an hour.

Example: in one night in 1955, Jo Ann Robinson ran off about 35,000 boycott handbills on a college mimeograph. The whole Montgomery Bus Boycott started on that machine.

Potluck

A shared meal where everyone brings a dish to pass, so there's no single host and no hierarchy, just a table built by the whole group.

A little history: American potlucks grew up in Protestant churches as a way to build cohesion, raise funds, and simply keep a community fed and together. In the Eastern US it's the "covered-dish supper."

Why it's in this course: in Appalachia the tradition runs deep, "dinner on the grounds" after church, covered-dish suppers at the fire hall or the family reunion. It's one of the oldest and strongest social media there is: no screen, high trust, and it holds a community together across generations. Exactly the "social" this course is trying to put back.
