1 · Communicative capitalism

In 2005, before the iPhone, Jodi Dean named the trap you felt Tuesday. Her claim: on networked platforms, your political speech stops being a message (something a person receives and answers) and becomes a contribution to circulation (one more unit added to the flow, valued only for moving). The foreclosure of politics is her name for the result: the energy that could have become action gets fully spent as circulation instead.

See it (a with / without pair)

Message: you email 200 neighbors, "The rezoning vote is Tuesday at 6pm at Town Hall, be there," and 40 people show up. It was received, answered, and acted on. It had use value.

Contribution: you post "SAY NO TO REZONING" to 30,000 strangers. It gets 2,000 likes and 400 shares. Nobody who liked it can attend, and no one has to. The likes are the product. It circulated beautifully and did nothing. Same passion, opposite effect.

Read the real thing (7–9 min)

Dean, "Communicative Capitalism: Circulation and the Foreclosure of Politics" (free PDF of the 2005 article). Read the opening pages and find her line about messages losing their communicative use and becoming just contributions to circulation. It's dense; read it slowly, one paragraph at a time.

"The message is simply part of a circulating data stream. Its particular content is irrelevant. Who sent it is irrelevant. Who receives it is irrelevant. That it need be responded to is irrelevant. The only thing that is relevant is circulation."
Your turn: find one thing your cause posted in the last month that was pure contribution, no one had to receive it, no one had to answer, nothing changed. Write what it would have taken to make it a message instead.
Compare with one sharp reading
A strong answer names the missing uptake, rather than "we should get more engagement." Example: "Our black-square solidarity post asked nothing of anyone and reached no decision-maker. A message version: a templated email our followers could send, with one click, to the specific council member whose vote we needed. Same feeling, but now it lands on someone accountable for a reply."

2 · The attention economy, and Jenny Odell

If content is infinite and free, the scarce thing is your attention. The economist Herbert Simon saw it in 1971: "a wealth of information creates a poverty of attention." The platform isn't selling you a service. You are the product, and your attention is the raw material. That reframes the whole problem: what would it mean to take yours back?

That's the question Jenny Odell takes up. Her "doing nothing" is not a digital detox and it is not apathy. It's two moves at once: refuse to hand your attention to the machine that converts it into circulation, and re-place that attention on something real, a place, a community, the actual people and living things in front of you. Reclaimed attention is the ground everything else stands on. You cannot organize what you can't even notice.

Hold the nuance: this is not "social media bad, touch grass good." Odell would be the first to say withdrawal-as-apathy is its own failure. The sharp version, hers and Dean's together: stop feeding the flow, and aim the same energy at things that produce uptake and durable relationship. Refusal in place, not escape. Attention re-placed on your own bioregion and neighbors is where real organizing has always started.
Read the real thing (8–10 min)

Odell, "How to Do Nothing" (the 2017 talk that became the book) (free full transcript). Read from the rose-garden opening through her account of what attention is for. Watch how she treats attention as a shared, almost civic resource, not a personal productivity hack.

"What the tech writer Jonathan Crary calls 'the attention economy' … it's a violence against the human capacity to reflect. And reflection is exactly the thing that this culture, at this moment, cannot afford to lose."
Your turn: name one place your own attention gets mined every day (the app, the feed, the notification), and one real thing you could re-place it on that would actually help your cause. Be concrete about both.
Compare with one sharp reading
A strong answer re-places attention on something that builds a relationship or a condition, rather than "read more news." Example: "I doom-scroll local outrage for an hour. Re-placed: I go to the actual county commission meeting once a month, where the decisions I'm angry about are made in a room I can stand in. Odell's move, aimed at the place the power actually is."

3 · Circulation or action?

Two judgments per case, and the obvious call is sometimes wrong. First, does the activity lean circulation, action, or a genuine mix? Second, what's the honest read of what it's really doing? Commit both, then Check.
1. A post denouncing a bad policy gets 50,000 shares. Nothing else is attached to it, no ask, no channel, no follow-up.
Leans
Read
Pure circulation. 50,000 shares, zero uptake. It moved through the stream and asked nothing of anyone. Dean's exact case: the content circulated, and that was the whole event.
2. A silly viral video challenge sweeps every feed for a month. Baked into it: a one-tap link that has routed real donations into a fund that actually paid for a research breakthrough. (Think the Ice Bucket Challenge.)
Leans
Read
Mixed, and this is the one where the obvious "it's just slacktivism" call is wrong. The form is pure circulation, but someone wired a conversion channel to it, so the reach actually cashed out as funded research. Circulation is not automatically empty; it's empty when nothing catches it.
3. A cause emails its 200-person list one specific ask, "call this office Tuesday and say these two sentences," and 60 people actually call.
Leans
Read
Action. Tiny reach, high uptake. 60 real calls land on someone who has to reckon with them. This is what a message looks like: received, answered, acted on. Nobody counts the "likes" because there aren't any; there are 60 phone calls.
4. A cause spends its entire budget boosting one video to 300,000 strangers in the region. Views spike. There's no email capture, no follow-up, no way to reach anyone again.
Leans
Read
Pure circulation, and the priciest kind. 300,000 rented views that vanish when the money stops, with no channel to hold anyone. This is a whole budget spent buying the feeling of reach. Compare it to case 3: 60 calls beat 300,000 forgotten views.
5. A cause runs a monthly in-person repair café. Fifteen regulars come, deep trust builds, people organize real help for each other. Almost no one outside the room knows it exists.
Leans
Read
Action, but starved of reach. This is the honest flip side: action is not automatically "better." The trust here is the real thing a viral post can never build, and it's invisible. The fix isn't to stop; it's to wire a little reach to it (a callback to Week 2's fast plus slow), so the strong core can grow.
6. An online petition hits 80,000 signatures. It looks like real action, it has a demand and a number. But no decision-maker has agreed to receive it, and there's no plan to make them.
Leans
Read
Pure circulation wearing action's clothes. A petition looks like uptake because it has a demand, but with no one obligated to receive or answer it, 80,000 signatures are 80,000 contributions to a stream. A petition becomes action only when it's attached to a person who must respond and a plan to make them.

4 · The case that spent 100 million views on nothing

Kony 2012

In March 2012, an advocacy group released a 30-minute film urging the world to help capture a warlord, Joseph Kony. Kony 2012 hit roughly 100 million views in six days, the fastest-spreading video to that point in history. It felt like a movement had happened overnight. Then almost nothing followed. Kony was never captured. The planned real-world action ("Cover the Night") mostly evaporated. The organization was badly criticized and soon fell apart. The circulation was total; the action was near zero.

Hold it against case 2 in the tool. The Ice Bucket Challenge looked just as frivolous, yet it wired a donation channel to the reach and funded a real scientific discovery. Same era, same virality. One converted circulation into action; the other spent it. That difference is the whole lesson, and the whole of Project 2.

Your turn: Kony 2012 had more reach than your cause will ever get, and produced less than 60 phone calls would. In one sentence, what was it missing that would have turned that ocean of attention into something real?
Compare with one sharp reading
"It had no channel to catch the attention and no local structure to hand it to, so 100 million people felt something for a week and then the feeling, having nowhere to go, became the whole event." Reach with no conversion and no organization behind it is just very loud circulation.

Your cause

The real, low-traction cause you chose in Week 1 to carry through the whole course. Its obscurity is the point: your media work can actually move the needle.

Communicative capitalism

Jodi Dean's term (2005) for a system where the sheer volume of our messaging becomes a form of capitalism in itself. Your political speech gets valued for how much it circulates, not for whether anyone receives, answers, or acts on it.

Example: a hashtag trends worldwide for two days, generates millions of posts, and changes no policy and no vote. The trend, not the outcome, was the product.

Jodi Dean

American political theorist (born 1962), professor at Hobart and William Smith Colleges. Her essay "Communicative Capitalism" (2005) argued, years before the like button ruled everything, that networked communication can absorb political energy and convert it into harmless circulation.

About Jodi Dean →

The foreclosure of politics

Dean's phrase for what communicative capitalism does: it doesn't ban politics, it uses it up. The passion that could have become organizing gets spent as posting, so the political possibility is quietly closed off ("foreclosed") while everyone feels active.

Example: you spend the evening arguing a cause online, go to bed exhausted and satisfied, and nothing in the world has moved. The feeling of participation replaced the effect of it.

Use value vs. exchange value

An old distinction Dean borrows. A thing's use value is the actual job it does (a coat keeps you warm). Its exchange value is what it's worth traded in a market (the coat sells for $80). Dean's move: online, a political message loses its use value (did anyone act on it?) and keeps only its exchange value (how much did it circulate?).

Example: "Vote Tuesday, here's your polling place" has use value, it helps someone act. A viral "DEMOCRACY IS DYING" post has exchange value, it circulates, whether or not a single person votes because of it.

The attention economy

The idea that in a world of unlimited information, human attention is the truly scarce resource, so it becomes the thing companies compete to capture and sell. Free apps aren't charities; they sell your attention to advertisers.

Example: an infinite-scroll feed is engineered to hold your eyes as long as possible, because every extra minute is inventory it can sell. You are not the customer; you're the product.

What the attention economy is →

Herbert Simon

Economist and cognitive scientist (1916–2001), a Nobel laureate. In 1971 he wrote the line the whole attention economy runs on: "a wealth of information creates a poverty of attention." He saw the trade-off decades before the feed made it a business model.

Jenny Odell

Oakland-based artist and writer. Her book How to Do Nothing: Resisting the Attention Economy (2019) grew from a 2017 talk. Her argument: reclaiming your attention from the feed, and re-placing it on real places and people, is a form of repair and even resistance, not laziness.

About Jenny Odell →

"Doing nothing"

Odell's deliberately provocative name for a practice: refusing to spend your attention on things designed to harvest it, and putting it instead on the slow, unproductive-looking things (a place, a community, sustained thought) that everything real is built from. It is not opting out of the world; it's opting back into the part of it that isn't for sale.

Example: instead of an hour scrolling local outrage, an hour at the actual neighborhood meeting where those decisions get made.

Bioregionalism

Paying attention to the living system you actually live in, your watershed, your soil, your local plants and food and weather, rather than an abstract "everywhere" online. Odell uses it to make attention concrete: know your place before you try to save the world.

Appalachian tie: this is native ground here. Knowing your holler and your creek, saving seeds, foraging ramps in spring, keeping a family foodway alive, the New River is one of the oldest rivers on earth and runs right through this region. That rooted, place-based attention (the same instinct behind Slow Food and regenerative farming) is exactly the kind Odell says the feed erodes. Re-placing attention on your bioregion is a very old Appalachian practice with a new name.

What bioregionalism is →

"Leans," not a box

Real activity rarely sits at a pure extreme. Most of it leans one way while carrying a little of the other. The skill isn't slapping on a label; it's seeing which way the weight falls and what the honest read is underneath.

The Ice Bucket Challenge

A 2014 viral trend where people filmed themselves dumping ice water on their heads to raise awareness of ALS, a fatal neurological disease, and nominated friends to do the same.

Why it's here: it looked like the definition of empty slacktivism, yet it raised over $100 million and helped fund the discovery of a gene tied to ALS. The trick was a donation channel wired directly to the reach. Proof that circulation isn't automatically hollow, it's hollow when nothing catches it.

Kony 2012

A 30-minute advocacy film released in March 2012 by the group Invisible Children, calling for the capture of Ugandan warlord Joseph Kony. It hit about 100 million views in six days, the fastest-spreading video of its time.

What happened next: almost nothing. Kony was never caught, the "Cover the Night" real-world action fizzled, and the organization collapsed amid criticism. The textbook case of total circulation and near-zero action, communicative capitalism in one clip.

The Kony 2012 story →

Slacktivism

A dismissive mashup of "slacker" and "activism": low-effort online gestures (a like, a shared graphic, a profile-frame) that make you feel involved while asking almost nothing and changing almost nothing.

Careful, though: the term can be too blunt. As the Ice Bucket case shows, a "slacktivist" gesture wired to a real channel can convert into action. The useful question isn't "is this slacktivism," it's "does anything catch it?"

What slacktivism means →

The society of the spectacle

An idea from French theorist Guy Debord (1967): in modern life, real relationships and actions get replaced by their images, we relate to representations of life instead of living it. Dean's "communicative capitalism" is a direct descendant, updated for the feed.

Example: attending a protest mainly to post that you were there, until the image of participating matters more than the participating. Debord saw the shape of this fifty years early.

About The Society of the Spectacle →

Repair café

A recurring free gathering where volunteers help neighbors fix broken things, lamps, bikes, clothes, instead of tossing them. The point is as much the connection as the repair: people meet, teach, and build trust over a shared table.

Example: a monthly repair café at a library becomes the room where a neighborhood actually knows each other, the kind of slow, high-trust "social" this course keeps pointing back to.

What a repair café is →

Getting out the vote (GOTV)

The work of turning people who agree with you into people who actually act (classically, vote). Field experiments found a striking pattern: a real face-to-face conversation moves people far more than mailers, ads, or robocalls.

Example: researchers Gerber and Green found door-to-door canvassing raised turnout several points, while mass mailings barely moved it. The most powerful medium turned out to be a trusted person. Next week's whole idea.
