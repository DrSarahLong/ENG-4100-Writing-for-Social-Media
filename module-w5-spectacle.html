Arguing Without Words

Tuesday you read the argument out of a picture. Today you learn how suffragists engineered it, judge what design moves actually do, and compose one for your own cause.

Tuesday, in one line: before anyone read a banner, the image of Inez Milholland in white robes on a white horse had already argued "we are dignified, and we belong." That is visual rhetoric. Staging it huge enough that the press had to cover it is spectacle. Today we take both apart and put them to work.

1 · Design is the argument

We tend to think an argument lives in words, and the picture just illustrates it. Design historian Teal Triggs flips that. Studying protest posters, punk zines, and movement graphics, she shows that the look of a thing, its color, its typeface, its handmade roughness or its polish, is itself making a claim about who these people are and what they want. The visual is not the wrapper on the argument. It is part of the argument.

See it: with words vs. with design
With words: "Suffragists are respectable women, not the wild-eyed cranks the newspapers caricature." A reader can agree or argue back.

With design: thousands of marchers in clean white, moving in calm order, led by a woman who looks like a classical statue of Liberty. There's nothing to argue back to. The picture has already made "respectable and dignified" feel like a fact. That is why the suffragists spent so much on color and costume: the design settled the question before the debate started.
Read the real thing (7–9 min)

"Imagery and Propaganda" (History of U.S. Woman's Suffrage) (free, open). Short, image-rich. Read for HOW color and symbols were used, the gold and yellow, the purple-white-gold sashes, the "Votes for Women" buttons, and the "Jailed for Freedom" pin. Notice they treated color like a brand.

Your turn: pick ONE design element from the reading (a color, a sash, a pin, a slogan set in a certain style). In one sentence, what argument did it make without stating it?
Compare with one sharp reading
A strong answer names the element AND the unstated claim. Example: "One shared color across every sash and banner argued 'we are one movement, not a scattering of cranks.' Uniformity itself was the message: this is organized, and it is everywhere."

2 · Spectacle: engineering the picture the press can't ignore

On March 3, 1913, Alice Paul put more than 5,000 marchers down Pennsylvania Avenue the day before Woodrow Wilson's inauguration, when the capital was already full of reporters. Color-coded sections. State floats. A giant Liberty Bell. An allegorical pageant on the Treasury steps with women costumed as Columbia, Justice, and Liberty. It was theater built to be photographed and reprinted for free in every paper in the country. That is spectacle: not a stunt for its own sake, but staging your argument at a scale the media has to carry.

See it: the spectacle did the distribution
A newspaper in 1913 could ignore a petition or a speech. It could not ignore 5,000 women, floats, and a golden-crowned herald on a white horse shutting down the capital's main avenue. The suffragists did not buy that coverage. The design of the event earned it. Spectacle turns your opponents' own media into your printing press.

Then the day turned. A hostile crowd surged into the street, jeering, spitting, grabbing and tripping marchers, while police mostly stood by. More than a hundred women were hurt; the Army cavalry was called in to clear the route. On the day it looked like a catastrophe. In print it did the opposite: the image of orderly women in white being mobbed made the marchers sympathetic and their attackers look like brutes. There were congressional hearings. The police chief lost his job. The spectacle absorbed even the violence and turned it into argument.

Read + look at the real thing (8–10 min)

"The Great Suffrage Parade of 1913" (U.S. National Park Service) (free, open). Read the sections on the floats, the pageant, and what happened to the marchers.

The official program of the procession (Library of Congress) (free primary source). Look at the cover: a woman on horseback blowing a herald's trumpet, a "Votes for Women" banner streaming behind, the Capitol at her back. Read the cover as an argument, the way you did Tuesday.

The plan: the artistic and allegorical beauty of the floats and dances would appeal to the eye, while the banners and transparencies would appeal to reason and a sense of justice. (paraphrased from the organizers' own program)
Your turn: the organizers split the work in two, beauty for the eye, argument for the mind. Why stage BOTH? What does the visual spectacle do that a banner full of reasons cannot, and vice versa?
Compare with one sharp reading
A strong answer treats them as a one-two punch. Example: "The image gets past your guard and earns attention no argument can buy. Then the banner tells you what to DO with the feeling, so you leave persuaded, not merely impressed. Spectacle without a claim is just a show; a claim without spectacle never gets seen."
Stay honest: the 1913 procession was also racially segregated. Fearing they would lose white Southern support, organizers asked Black suffragists to march at the back. A movement's visual rhetoric decides what the public sees, and also who is allowed to be IN the frame, and this one tried to keep Black women out of the picture. Brilliant design and real exclusion sat in the same parade. Both are true, and the next section is about the woman who refused to be cropped out.
Then and now: spectacle still runs the same play
You have seen Alice Paul's logic in your own feed. When Extinction Rebellion blocks a bridge with hundreds of bodies and a single stark hourglass symbol, or when marchers stage a silent, visually identical mass to be photographed from above, they are doing exactly what 1913 did: build one image so striking the press has to carry it, and let the picture make the argument before a word is read. A century later, the playbook holds.

3 · Read the design

Every design move is doing a job. Two judgments per case. First, what is this move PRIMARILY doing: Dignify (change how outsiders see the group), Unite (make members recognize each other and feel like one movement), or Disrupt (force the wider public to look through scale or shock)? Second, name the trade it makes. The obvious answer is sometimes wrong, and some cases do two things at once. Commit both, then Check.
1. Inez Milholland leads the 1913 march in flowing white robes and a golden crown, on a white horse.
Doing
Trade
Dignify. The white robes and classical crown answer the slander that suffragists were unwomanly cranks. The trade is respectability: leaning on one conventional (white, beautiful, ladylike) ideal makes the movement palatable, and quietly signals who "belongs" at the front. Ida B. Wells felt that edge directly.
2. Every marcher, thousands of them, wears the same yellow sash reading "Votes for Women."
Doing
Trade
Unite. One repeated color and slogan turns a crowd into a recognizable single body, and lets members find each other on any street after the march. Its main cost is reach: a sash mostly speaks to people already looking. It builds the "we" more than it converts the "them."
3. Later suffragists release images of themselves being force-fed during a hunger strike in prison to the press.
Doing
Trade
Disrupt. A brutal, unignorable image forces the public to reckon with what the state is doing to these women. The risk: a spectacle that shocking can become the whole story ("the force-feeding") and swallow the actual demand (the vote). Attention is captured; steering it is the hard part.
4. The allegorical pageant on the Treasury steps: women costumed as Columbia, Justice, and Liberty in flowing robes, releasing a dove.
Doing
Trade
The tempting answer is Disrupt, because a giant pageant is pure spectacle. But its rhetorical JOB is to Dignify: it recasts suffragists as the classical virtues of the Republic itself, Justice and Liberty in person, not agitators. Same respectability trade as #1, and the same blind spot about who gets cast as "Liberty." Spectacle can be a delivery system for a dignifying argument.
5. A present-day cause asks every supporter to add the same colored frame to their profile photo for a week.
Doing
Trade
Unite. It is the digital sash: a shared visual marker so supporters recognize each other and feel like a movement. It reaches mostly the already-convinced (your friends see your frame), and it can slide into pure identity display with no action behind it, a trap we name hard in Week 11.
6. A cause stages a die-in with fake blood on the courthouse steps, timed for the evening news.
Doing
Trade
Disrupt. Bodies on the ground and fake blood are engineered for the camera and refuse to be ignored. The trade here is the sharpest one: shock reaches far and fast, and it can also read as extreme to the undecided middle you most need. High risk, high reach. You'll meet the masters of this in the ACT UP week.

4 · Who gets to be in the picture

Ida B. Wells, 1913

Ida B. Wells, journalist, anti-lynching crusader, and founder of a Black women's suffrage club in Chicago, came to Washington to march with her Illinois delegation. Organizers, worried about white Southern support, told the Black suffragists to walk together at the back, out of the main image. Wells stepped off to the side. Then, as her Illinois delegation passed, she walked out of the crowd and took her place in it, in the picture, where she had earned the right to be. No speech. The act itself was the argument.

Every choice about a movement's spectacle is also a choice about who is visible in it. The 1913 procession made a dignified, unified image partly by trying to crop some of its own members out. Wells refused to be cropped. Visual rhetoric is never only about what you show. It is about who you let be seen.

Your turn: in one sentence, what did Wells's quiet act argue that a banner reading "include Black women" could not have?
Compare with one sharp reading
"By simply standing in the frame she belonged in, she made her inclusion a fact on the ground instead of a request, and forced everyone who saw the photo to see that the 'unified' movement had tried to divide itself." An act inside the image can argue what a caption outside it only asks for.

Visual rhetoric

The idea that an image, a color, a layout, or a designed object makes an argument, the same way words do. The visual persuades before, and sometimes instead of, the text.

Example: a stop sign is red and octagonal even before you read "STOP." The color and shape already argue "danger, halt." A movement in all-white argues "we are pure and dignified" with no caption.

Spectacle

Staging something so large, striking, or unusual that the media has to cover it, so the event distributes your message for you. Not a stunt for its own sake: spectacle with a clear demand behind it.

Example: 5,000 marchers, floats, and a costumed pageant shutting down Pennsylvania Avenue in 1913. No newspaper could ignore it, so the suffragists got national coverage they never had to buy.

Teal Triggs

British design historian and educator who studies graphic design as communication, especially DIY and protest design: punk fanzines, movement posters, and self-made publications. Her work argues that the look of a thing (its type, color, and roughness) carries meaning and identity, beyond the words on it.

Why she's here: she gives us the vocabulary to treat a poster or a sash as an argument to be analyzed, which is exactly what this week asks you to do.

Alice Paul

Suffragist (1885–1977) who organized the 1913 procession at age 28 and later founded the National Woman's Party. She had learned militant, media-savvy tactics from the British suffragettes and brought spectacle and confrontation into the American movement.

Example of her thinking: she picked the date (the eve of Wilson's inauguration) precisely because the press was already in town. Nothing about the event was accidental.

Inez Milholland

Suffragist, labor lawyer, and activist (1886–1916) chosen to lead the 1913 procession on a white horse in white robes and a crown, cast as a herald of the movement. She was picked in part because the press already called her "the most beautiful suffragist," which the organizers used as a weapon against the "ugly, unwomanly" stereotype.

The suffrage color code

Suffragists treated color like a brand. American groups used gold and yellow (from an early Kansas campaign and the sunflower); the National Woman's Party used purple, white, and gold. Supporters wore the colors on sashes, ribbons, and buttons so the movement was recognizable at a glance, anywhere.

Example: seeing three women in matching gold sashes on a train platform told everyone, with no words, "there is an organized movement, and it is here too."

Sash

A wide band of cloth worn across the body, printed with a slogan or color. In the suffrage movement, thousands of matching "Votes for Women" sashes turned a crowd into one visibly unified body and let members identify each other instantly.

Example: the modern equivalent is a shared campaign T-shirt or a colored profile-photo frame. Same job: make the group recognizable and make belonging visible.

Allegorical pageant

A staged tableau where performers embody abstract ideas as costumed figures, Liberty, Justice, Peace, Columbia, so the audience "sees" the concept as a person. An old form of visual argument, from medieval processions to civic parades.

Example: at the 1913 Treasury-steps pageant, a woman costumed as Justice in purple robes let the crowd literally see suffrage as the cause of Justice herself, not of "agitators." The costume made the claim.

Hunger strike

Refusing food while imprisoned to protest and to force a moral crisis on your jailers. Suffragists in the US and Britain used it; authorities responded with brutal force-feeding, and activists made sure images and accounts of it reached the press.

Why it's visual rhetoric: the point was partly the picture. An image of the state violently force-feeding a peaceful woman argued the movement's whole case about power in a single, unignorable frame.

Die-in

A protest where people lie on the ground as if dead, often for a set number of minutes, to make an abstract harm (deaths from AIDS, gun violence, traffic crashes) visible and impossible to walk past.

Example: ACT UP used die-ins during the AIDS crisis to force the public and the press to SEE the death toll that officials were treating as a statistic. You'll study ACT UP directly in Week 8.

Ida B. Wells

Journalist, educator, and civil-rights leader (1862–1931) famous for her investigative reporting against lynching. She founded the Alpha Suffrage Club, one of the first Black women's suffrage organizations, in Chicago.

In 1913: told to march at the back of the procession, she refused, waited in the crowd, and stepped into her Illinois state delegation as it passed, insisting on being seen where she belonged.

Segregation in the 1913 march

To avoid offending white Southern suffragists, organizers asked Black participants to march together at the back rather than with their state delegations. Some complied under protest; Ida B. Wells refused. It is a clear case of a movement's "unified" image being built by excluding some of its own members.

Why we name it: visual rhetoric decides who is in the frame. Studying the design with clear eyes means studying who it left out.

The quilting bee (and the banner-making circle)

A gathering where women sew together, one project passed among many hands, while news, plans, and grievances travel around the frame. The suffrage banners and sashes were often made this same way: collective handwork that produced the movement's visual materials AND organized the women making them.

A little history: in Appalachia, the quilting bee was one of the oldest and strongest social media there was, a slow, high-trust gathering where a mountain community shared information, made decisions, and held itself together across isolated hollers, all while making something durable with their hands. Long before a "feed," the quilting frame was where a community's real news circulated.

Why it's here: making the movement's visual materials was itself an act of organizing. The design and the community that made it were the same work.

Samizdat

Underground self-publishing in the Soviet Union and Eastern Bloc: banned writing typed or handwritten a few copies at a time, then passed through trusted networks, with each reader expected to make more copies. The name is a wry Russian coinage meaning roughly "self-published."

Example: a banned poem retyped on a typewriter with five carbon sheets, each copy passed to a friend who retyped five more. Slow, risky, and nearly impossible for censors to shut down, because the reader WAS the publisher.
