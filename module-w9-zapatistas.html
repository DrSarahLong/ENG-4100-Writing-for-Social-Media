Tuesday, in one line: a few thousand Zapatista farmers held off an army by pairing the slowest medium (a community assembly that decides over days) with the fastest (the early internet). Today we read their actual words, name the network that carried them, and turn the same lens on your cause.

1 · Read the communiqué

The Zapatistas' main weapon was writing. Not a rifle, a communiqué: a public dispatch mailed from the Lacandón jungle to newspapers, then typed onto the internet by volunteers within hours. Most of them came from one masked spokesman, Subcomandante Marcos, who wrote like a poet and a comedian more than a general.

See it
A government press release says: "The administration announces new measures regarding regional development." A Zapatista communiqué says: "We are a product of 500 years of struggle… But today, we say ¡Ya Basta!, Enough!" Same category of document, a public bulletin, but one is built to bore insiders and the other is built to make a stranger three countries away feel something.
Read the real thing (6–8 min)

First Declaration of the Lacandón Jungle (Radio Zapatista, free, Creative-Commons text). Read the opening and the list of demands. Notice how fast it moves from "500 years" to a plain list of ordinary needs.

"We are the inheritors of the true builders of our nation… we have nothing, absolutely nothing, not even a roof over our heads: no land, no work, no health care, no food, no education."
Your turn: the demand is written as a list of plain human needs (land, work, health care, food, education), not a political program. Why would a movement whose survival depends on the whole world watching write it that way?
Compare with one sharp reading
A strong answer names the audience. Example: "Jargon recruits people who already agree. A list of needs recruits strangers, because anyone can read 'no health care, no food' and feel it. When your defense is the world's attention, you write for the stranger, not the specialist."
Optional, if you want to hear the storyteller (5 min)

Marcos, "The Parrot and the Macaw" (struggle.ws, free full text). A short Marcos tale. This is the other half of the weapon: he didn't only declare, he told stories, so people kept reading the way you keep watching a series.

2 · The network that carried it

A communiqué is only paper until something moves it. What moved these was a solidarity network: volunteers who translated each dispatch and pushed it onto email lists and the young internet, so that a message written in a jungle with no phone line reached forty countries in days. When the army pressed its offensive, that global noise, faxes, embassy protests, headlines, helped force it to stop. Analysts gave the pattern a name: netwar.

See it
Picture the difference. A traditional army has a chain of command: knock out the general and it stalls. The Zapatista support network had no general. It was thousands of small groups, a listserv here, a student club there, a union local somewhere else, all acting on the same communiqué at once. There was no headquarters to raid. Scholar Harry Cleaver called it "an electronic fabric of struggle": pull one thread and the cloth holds.
Read the real thing (7–9 min)

Harry Cleaver, "The Zapatista Effect" (University of Texas, free full text). Read the opening section on how the internet turned local solidarity work into a worldwide fabric. Skip the dense economics if you like; you want the argument about the network.

"The computer networks… provided vehicles not only for the traditional work of solidarity but for the weaving of a new electronic fabric of struggle to carry the resistance."
Your turn: netwar's edge is that there's no head to cut off. Name one strength and one real weakness of a movement with no headquarters and no single leader. (Hold onto the weakness; it's the whole point of next week.)
Compare with one sharp reading
A strong answer sees the trade. Example: "Strength: it survives repression, there's no boss to jail. Weakness: it can't easily decide, change course, or negotiate, because there's no one with the authority to say yes. It can assemble a crowd overnight and then not know what to do with it." That weakness is exactly Tufekci's Week 10 argument.

3 · Read the audit

Project 2 is a content audit and strategy for your cause. An audit isn't a scolding list of "post more." It's a diagnosis: what is this cause's media presence actually missing, and what's the smartest first move to fix it? The Zapatistas are your model. They knew a viral message is empty without a real community behind it, and a real community gets crushed in silence without reach. Watch for that both/and here.

Six real-world audit snapshots. For each, make two calls. First: what's actually missing? Second: the smartest first move. The obvious reflex ("just post more") is almost never the right move. Commit both, then Check.
1. Your cause's posts average 2,000 views, but you have no email list, no group chat, no way to ever contact a single person who cared.
Missing
Move
Missing: a way to hold people. Two thousand views that vanish are reach with no relationship, the Zapatista trap in reverse. Build an owned channel (an email list, a group) so the people who care don't leak away. Numbers you can't contact again aren't an audience.
2. You post on five platforms. A stranger who reads all of them still can't tell what your cause actually wants or who it's for.
Missing
Move
Missing: a throughline. Five platforms and no clear message is just noise on more channels. The Zapatista "¡Ya Basta!" worked because it was one sharp thing a stranger could grasp instantly. Define one message and one audience before you post another word. More posting only spreads the confusion.
3. Your cause serves rural older adults. Ninety percent of your effort goes into short-video apps, where the people you actually serve almost never are.
Missing
Move
Missing: presence where your people are. Beautiful videos are worthless if your audience isn't in the room. The Zapatistas reached the world by the internet but reached their own base by church, radio, and word of mouth, the channels their people already used. Go where the affected people already gather, even if it's a printed flyer or a local radio slot.
4. One post hit 80,000 views. Traffic spiked for a day, then flatlined. There was no link, no ask, no follow-up anywhere on it.
Missing
Move
Missing: a next step. The reach worked; the on-ramp didn't exist, so 80,000 people arrived at a door with no handle. The tempting move is to chase another viral hit. The smart move is to make sure the next spike converts: one clear ask or link on everything, so attention turns into a name on a list, a signature, a person who shows up.
5. Fifteen volunteers meet every month and do real, sustained work. None of it is documented anywhere online, so to an outsider your cause looks dead.
Missing
Move
Missing: visibility for a strong slow core. You have the rarest thing in this whole course, a real bonded community, and it's invisible. This is the Zapatista lesson exactly: the assembly was the strength, the internet just let the world see it. Start documenting the offline work (photos, short notes, a monthly recap) so the network can see itself and strangers can find the real thing.
6. You have a small, loyal newsletter (200 devoted readers) and steady monthly meetings. You've never once tried to reach anyone new. Growth is flat and the group is quietly aging out.
Missing
Move
Missing: any reach to new people. This is case 1 flipped. Here the slow, high-trust core is strong and there's zero fast reach, so it's dying quietly. The fix isn't "build more trust," you have that. It's the missing half of the Zapatista pairing: add one fast-reach move (a public explainer, a shareable clip, one open event) to introduce the strong core to strangers. Diagnosis first, then the move follows.

4 · Twelve days that ran on words

Chiapas, January 1994

On January 1, 1994, the Zapatistas seized several towns in Chiapas. The Mexican army answered with overwhelming force. By any military math, this was over. What broke the math was the network: within days, communiqués were translated and posted worldwide, solidarity groups flooded embassies and newsrooms, and the government found it could not press a quiet offensive against people the whole world was now reading about. Around twelve days in, it declared a ceasefire and, remarkably, sat down to talk.

Notice the pairing. The slow assembly built a real community worth defending. The fast network made sure the world watched them defend it. Neither alone would have worked: a viral post with no community behind it is empty, and a real community nobody hears about gets crushed in silence.

Stay honest. Don't romanticize the ending. The Zapatistas never won their formal demands, the state response stayed harsh, and in 1997 a paramilitary group massacred 45 people at Acteal, most of them women and children. What they won was survival, self-rule in their own territory, and a playbook every networked movement since has borrowed. Both things are true at once.
Your turn: the ceasefire came because the world was watching, not because the Zapatistas could win a battle. In one sentence, what did the slow assembly give the movement that the fast network never could?
Compare with one sharp reading
"It gave them something real to broadcast, a bonded community that had already decided, together, to hold the line, so that when the world tuned in there was a genuine thing to defend rather than a hashtag." The network amplifies; it can't manufacture the thing being amplified.

The Zapatistas (EZLN)

The Zapatista Army of National Liberation, mostly Maya farmers in Chiapas, Mexico, named for Emiliano Zapata of the Mexican Revolution. They rose up on January 1, 1994, the day the NAFTA trade deal took effect.

Example: instead of trying to seize the capital, they seized the world's attention, and turned a local uprising into a global cause.

Subcomandante Marcos

The masked, pipe-smoking spokesman who wrote most of the Zapatista communiqués. "Subcomandante," sub-commander, was deliberate: he insisted he served the indigenous command, not the reverse. A former professor who said he had to learn to listen for years before he earned the right to speak.

Example: asked to reveal his "real" identity, he answered that he was every excluded person everywhere. The refusal to have one face was the point.

Communiqué

A public dispatch or bulletin issued by a group to the world (say "kuh-MEW-nih-kay"). Armies and governments issue dry ones; the Zapatistas made them literary, part manifesto, part letter, part story.

Example: the First Declaration of the Lacandón Jungle was a communiqué, written in the jungle, mailed to newspapers, then posted online and translated into dozens of languages within days.

What a communiqué is →

Netwar

Conflict waged by a swarm of small, networked groups rather than one chain of command. Coined by analysts John Arquilla and David Ronfeldt at the RAND think tank, who studied the Zapatistas as the first clear case.

Example: no general to capture, no headquarters to raid. Thousands of scattered solidarity groups all acted on the same communiqué at once, which made the movement almost impossible to shut down by force.

Netwar explained →

Solidarity network

People who don't share your location or your exact struggle but choose to act on your behalf, spreading your message, showing up, applying pressure from afar. For the Zapatistas, this was the fast half of the strategy: strangers worldwide carrying a jungle communiqué in days.

A little history + a Southern tie: long before the internet, the coalfields of West Virginia and eastern Kentucky ran on solidarity networks. In the 1921 Battle of Blair Mountain, thousands of miners tied red bandanas around their necks so they could recognize each other across a scattered uprising, one likely root of the word "redneck." A distributed movement with no single head, held together by a shared sign. Same logic the Zapatistas would run on the wire seventy years later.

Harry Cleaver

An economist at the University of Texas who tracked the Zapatista support network in the 1990s. He named the way computer networks turned scattered solidarity into a worldwide "electronic fabric of struggle," an early, prescient read on internet organizing.

¡Ya Basta!

Spanish for "Enough!" The rallying cry of the 1994 uprising. Two words that anyone anywhere can feel, which is exactly why they traveled.

Example: a stranger who knows nothing about Chiapas still understands "Enough." That instant grasp is what let the phrase jump borders.

The community assembly

How the Zapatistas make decisions: the community talks a question through, sometimes for days, until it reaches consensus, rather than taking an order from the top. Slow on purpose.

Example: a decision the whole community shaped is one the whole community will actually carry out, and there's no single boss to arrest, because the authority lives in everyone.

Mandar obedeciendo

"To lead by obeying." The Zapatista principle that leaders carry out what the community's assembly decides, instead of the community obeying the leader.

Example: it's why capturing Marcos wouldn't have ended the movement. He was a spokesman for a decision made by many, not the decider.

Chiapas

Mexico's southernmost state, mountainous, heavily indigenous, and among its poorest. The Zapatista base. The gap between its resource wealth and its people's poverty was central to the uprising.

The Lacandón jungle

The rainforest region of eastern Chiapas where the Zapatistas organized and from which the communiqués were "issued." Naming the jungle as the dateline was itself a move: it located the words in a real, rooted place.

Marcos's storytelling

The communiqués weren't only declarations. Marcos wrote recurring characters: Old Antonio, a Mayan elder who teaches through fable, and Durito, a talking beetle who fancies himself a knight-errant and lectures Marcos on politics.

Example: people who didn't care about Mexican policy read the Durito tales like a comic serial, and absorbed the politics anyway. Story was the sugar on the argument.

Content audit

A clear-eyed inventory of what a cause or organization is actually putting out, where, for whom, and to what effect, so you can see what's missing before you plan what's next.

Example: "We post daily on three apps, but we have no email list, no clear ask, and our real offline work is invisible." That sentence is an audit. It tells you exactly where to start.
