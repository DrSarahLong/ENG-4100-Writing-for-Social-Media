1 · Small is all, and it is a production instruction

adrienne maree brown published Emergent Strategy in 2017, drawing on the science-fiction worlds of Octavia Butler and on two decades of organizing in Detroit. Her central claim is that large-scale change emerges from the quality of small-scale relationships rather than being scaled down from a plan. She calls the pattern fractal: what you build at the smallest level repeats at every level above it.

Most people read that as a philosophy. This week it is a production schedule, and it says test the one before you make the many.

The instruction it gives you
You built one over the weekend. Put that one in front of five people and watch what they do with it with no explanation from you, revise it based on what they get wrong, and then produce the run.

The reason is practical. A badly-designed artifact you made one of wastes an evening. A badly-designed artifact you made two hundred of wastes the project, and you find out when you are standing in front of the class explaining why nobody took one.

What the five-person test actually catches

Imagine a zine folded from one sheet, eight panels, handed to people waiting for the bus. You make one and hand it to five people you know. Here is the sort of thing that comes back, and none of it is visible on a screen.

Two of the five open it upside down, because the fold order is not obvious and you knew the fold order. One cannot read the 10-point type in the shade at the bus stop. One asks what they are supposed to do with it, which means your ask is on panel seven and should be on panel two. One puts it in a pocket, which tells you the thing people actually want is small, and your next version should be smaller.

Five people, twenty minutes, and you now redesign before you print, not after.

Read alongside (8–10 min)

Emergent Strategy (Commons Social Change Library, summarizing brown's framework). Read it with your own artifact in mind, and pick the one principle that changes a decision you were about to make.

Unleashing Creative Campaigns (Center for Artistic Activism, 2025, free). Read the prototyping section on PDF page 62, which is the make-one-first rule in their words, and run the Creative Campaign Checklist on page 9 against your own build plan.

The complication: “move at the speed of trust” is sound advice and also a convenient excuse for missing a deadline. You have three weeks. Emergent strategy says build small and let it grow; it does not say wait until it feels ready. Decide today.

2 · From one unit to the whole run

You spec'd the object last week. Three decisions are still open, and they are the ones that decide what the artifact means rather than what it looks like.

Who makes it

You alone, or the people who receive it? This is the biggest unanswered question in most Project 3 drafts, and it changes the argument completely. An object you made is a statement. An object two hundred people each added one piece to is evidence.Bell to Bell: the stamp is the organizer's; the line on each card is the writer's. That split is what makes the stack count for something.

How the run actually gets produced

Not how many, which you already decided, but when and by whose hands. Two hundred of anything is several hours. Block the hours on a calendar or it does not happen.Two evenings of stamping, forty minutes a batch of fifty, plus an hour to cut the slot and letter the box.

Where it ends up

Every artifact has an afterlife, and it is usually the strongest part of the campaign. What happens to the thing when the three weeks are over?The whole stack is banded and carried by hand into the board's public-comment meeting, and held up while one person speaks.
Your turn: answer all three. Write them as decisions, not options, and put the production hours on a day.
Compare with one strong reading
A strong answer makes the who-makes-it split do work. "WHO: the sign is entirely mine, and that is the point, because one person standing there with something they obviously painted themselves reads differently than a printed banner from a campaign. But the back of it is blank and I am bringing a marker, so anybody who stops can add a near-miss they have had. PRODUCTION: one Saturday afternoon, four hours, including drying time I forgot about the first time. AFTERLIFE: it goes to the town council meeting on the 18th, and after that it lives in the department hallway." A weak answer says "I'll make it, and then I guess I'll keep it."

3 · Access is a design decision

Project 3 asks you to say how you handled at least three of the seven access questions below. The common wrong answer, and we might as well get it out of the way, is that a physical object has no accessibility obligations because it is not a website. The opposite is closer to true. A website has standards, tooling, and a checker. A table in an atrium has a step, a font size, a noise level, and a forty-minute time commitment, and nobody is going to flag any of it for you.

Digital accessibility still applies to whatever you post (alt text, captions, contrast, readable type). These seven are the analog set.

Physical access. Is the location step-free, with a route that avoids stairs, gravel, and curbs? Can somebody seated reach the thing? Example: the listening booth moved from the library steps to the ground-floor atrium, and the table dropped to 30 inches.
Print and sight. Type at 14pt or larger, high contrast, no text over a busy photograph, and a large-print version available. Example: ten of the 200 zines were printed at 18pt.
Language. Plain language at roughly an eighth-grade level, and translation where your audience needs it. Example: 40 of the cards were printed in Spanish because about a third of the complex speaks it at home.
Time, seating, and stamina. Somewhere to sit, a two-minute way to take part as well as the forty-minute way, and no requirement to stand in a line. Example: the procession had a short route option and two chairs at the end.
Sensory load. No sudden noise or strobing, a quieter time slot, and a warning when part of it is loud. Example: the last thirty minutes of the booth were posted as a quiet hour.
Touch and non-visual access. Can a blind participant take part at all? Example: the cards were rubber-stamped so the number could be felt, and a volunteer read the zine aloud on request.
A route in for people who cannot be there. Presence is the point of an analog artifact, so say how somebody who could not come still took part. Example: eleven people mailed in a card, and somebody else carried theirs.
A sentence in the report is not the deliverable
Every example above is a change somebody made to a thing rather than a line they added to a document. Access decisions change the object: where the table goes, what size the type is, whether there is a chair to sit in.

4 · Find what is missing

Seven campaigns, each one well-built and each one shutting somebody out. Pick which of the seven access questions went unanswered. Some could arguably be two; pick the one that excludes the most people in that specific setup, and the feedback will tell you why.

The quilt square drive

A table where people stitch a square for a collective banner. It is set up on the top landing of the stone steps outside the old library, up six steps from the quad, because that is where the light is best for photographs.

The listening booth

Two chairs and a volunteer who records a neighbor's story about the rent increase. Each session runs about forty minutes, by appointment, and there is a sign-up sheet on a clipboard with a line of people waiting their turn.

The bus-stop zine

A one-sheet zine, eight panels, handed to people waiting at Plemmons. The design is good: 9-point type set over a full-bleed photograph of the crossing at dusk, white letters on the image.

The comment wall

Two hundred index cards hung at eye height across a walkway, each carrying one handwritten line. A pen and blank cards sit on a table so people can add their own, and a small printed sign explains the prompt.

The call-and-response action

A short call-and-response performance in the stairwell atrium at noon, with a bullhorn and a drum, chosen because the enclosed space carries sound and the noise is the point.

The letter-writing table

A table in the apartment complex's community room with pens, stamped envelopes, and a one-page handout explaining what to write. The handout is 14pt, high contrast, well laid out, and written in English at about a college reading level. Roughly a third of the complex speaks Spanish at home.

The silent procession

A hundred and ninety hand-lettered cards carried in a single file line into a council meeting, one card per person, each carried by the person who wrote it. The route is step-free and short, there are chairs at the end, and the lettering is large.

Your turn: run the seven questions on your own artifact and write down the three you are going to answer, as changes to the object. Then write the one you are knowingly not solving, and why.
Why saying which one you skipped is the stronger answer
Because a report that claims all seven were handled is either unusual or untrue, and the rubric rewards an accurate account over a flattering one. "We could not translate the handout. A third of the complex speaks Spanish at home, so a third of our audience got a worse version of this, and the next round starts by finding somebody who can check a translation" is a stronger paragraph than seven checkmarks. It also gives you element 7, Iteration, for free.

5 · The case: what production failure looks like

Chalk in October

A campaign about a 40% rent increase at a 240-unit complex chalked the numbers on the sidewalk outside the leasing office: the old rent, the new rent, the difference, repeated down the walk so you read it as you approached the door. The imagery was good, it cost nothing, and it was difficult to argue with.

It rained that night. By morning there was nothing, and almost nobody had seen it.

Their report says it outright: “The chalk was gone in one rain and almost nobody saw it, which was a design failure we should have predicted.” And the iteration: “Chalk was wrong for October. Next time the same information goes on a laminated card zip-tied to the mailbox cluster, where it stays dry and people stand still for thirty seconds anyway.”

The complication: this is not an argument against chalk. Chalk is excellent for a one-hour action you will photograph, and the Copenhagen result says the creative version usually outperforms the conventional one. The failure was matching a material that lasts four hours to a goal that needed three weeks. Durability is a design decision, and it belongs in your five answers above.
Your turn: what is your version of the rain? Say what could physically destroy, remove, or quietly nullify your artifact, and what you are doing about it.

Octavia Butler

Pathbreaking American science-fiction writer (1947–2006), one of the first Black women to win the field's top awards. Her Parable novels imagine communities surviving societal collapse by adapting and cooperating instead of dominating.

Why she's here: brown built much of Emergent Strategy on Butler's line "the only lasting truth is change," turning a novelist's vision into an organizing practice.

Fractal

A shape or pattern that repeats at every scale: zoom in or out and you see the same form again.

Example: a fern (each small frond is the shape of the whole leaf), a coastline, a branching tree, a snowflake. brown's version for people: "how we are at the small scale is how we are at the large scale," so a two-person conversation already carries the shape of the whole movement.

What a fractal is →

Move at the speed of trust

One of brown's principles: relationships have their own pace, and you cannot force them faster. If a movement grows faster than the trust holding it together, it tears.

Example: asking a first-time follower to donate or organize is outrunning trust. A smaller first ask (come to one low-stakes thing) lets trust build until a bigger ask can hold.

Emergence

When a big, complex pattern arises from many small, simple interactions, with no one in charge of the whole. The parts follow local rules; the order appears on its own.

Example: a flock of starlings turning as one (no lead bird), or a market price, or a language. No one designs these from the top; they emerge from the small parts relating.

Plain language

A writing standard with specific rules: short sentences, common words, active voice, one idea per sentence, and the reader's action stated early. Roughly an eighth-grade reading level is the usual target for public-facing material, and US federal agencies have been required to write this way since the Plain Writing Act of 2010.

With and without:

Without: “Residents wishing to express concerns regarding the proposed adjustment to lease terms may avail themselves of the public comment period.”
Plain: “You can tell the board what you think about the rent increase. The meeting is Tuesday at 6. You get three minutes.”

The Maker Space

App State's shared fabrication shop, which you have access to as a student. Depending on the facility that usually means laser cutting and engraving, 3D printing, vinyl cutting, sewing machines, large-format printing, screen printing, button making, and hand tools.

What to find out this week, because it decides your timeline: which machines need a safety orientation before they will let you touch one, when those orientations run, what you pay for materials, and how far ahead you have to reserve time. A laser cutter you cannot book until the week after you needed it is the same as no laser cutter.

A note on craft: a hand-cut, hand-painted object and a laser-cut one carry different information. Visible handwork is part of the argument in a craftivist campaign, so machine precision is not automatically the better choice.
