1 · Emergence, and "small is all"

In 2017, an organizer and writer named adrienne maree brown published a book called Emergent Strategy. Her core move: stop borrowing your strategy from war and advertising (conquer, scale, dominate), and start borrowing it from living systems (adapt, connect, grow from the small). A lot of it she draws from the science-fiction writer Octavia Butler, who imagined communities that survive collapse by bending instead of breaking.

See it
Her most-quoted line: "Small is good, small is all. The large is a reflection of the small." Picture a compost bin and a whole regenerative farm. The farm is only ever as healthy as the soil life in one handful of that compost, multiplied. You cannot fake healthy soil at scale. You build it small, and the large becomes a reflection of it. brown says movements work the same way: the big thing carries the character of its smallest parts, for better or worse.
Read the real thing (7–9 min)

"Emergent Strategy" (The Commons Social Change Library) (free, open access). Read the "Core Principles" and "Elements" lists slowly. This is the plainest map of brown's whole framework.

"Small is good, small is all. (The large is a reflection of the small.) … Focus on critical connections more than critical mass, build the resilience by building the relationships."
Your turn: read the list of principles. Pick the ONE that most argues with how you have been running your cause so far. Name it, and say what it would change.
Compare with one sharp reading
A strong answer names a real tension, not a compliment to yourself. Example: "'What you pay attention to grows' argues with me. I spend my energy tracking follower counts, so follower counts are what grow, not relationships. It would mean pointing my attention at the twelve people who already show up, and watching that grow instead."

2 · Fractal, and the speed of trust

brown's key word is fractal: how we are at the small scale is how we are at the large scale. The way two people treat each other in your campaign is the way ten thousand people will experience it later, scaled up. Which leads to her most practical rule: move at the speed of trust. You cannot rush a relationship, and if you try to grow faster than trust can hold, the whole thing tears.

See it (a with / without pair)
Without the fractal lens: "Our public image is warm and welcoming, so we're fine," while behind the scenes the two organizers snipe at each other and burn out every volunteer in a month. With the fractal lens: you see that the private sniping IS the movement's true character, and it will surface publicly the second you scale. The small is the tell. Fix the two-person dynamic and you are fixing the future ten-thousand-person one.

Instead of critical mass (a big number of loosely-attached people), brown points at critical connections (a few strong relationships that can actually bear weight). A crowd scatters when the moment passes. A web of trust holds. And the whole thing grows less like an ad campaign and more like mycelium: mostly underground, relational, decentralized, with no single point of failure.

Read the real thing (6–8 min)

"Earning Our Place on the Planet: An Interview with adrienne maree brown" (Longreads) (free, open access). brown in her own voice on emergence, trust, and change. Read for how she talks, warm, slow, unhurried, the tone is part of the argument.

"When two different ways of being come into contact with each other there's also an opportunity there to generate a new way of being … that neither one person could have come up with on their own."
Your turn: where in your campaign are you moving faster than trust can hold? Name one place you are asking people for more than the relationship can currently carry.
Compare with one sharp reading
A strong answer names the specific ask and the missing trust behind it. Example: "I keep asking people to 'commit to organizing' after one Instagram comment. I'm asking for marriage on a first date. The speed-of-trust move is a much smaller first ask, come to one low-stakes thing, and let the trust build before I ask for their time."

3 · The emergent read

Two calls per case, and the obvious answer is sometimes the wrong one. First: does this move try to grow by mass (numbers, reach) or by connection (relationships)? Some are genuinely mixed. Second: name the emergent read, what brown would actually say is happening. Commit both, then Check.
1. Two weeks before a big event, a cause spends its whole budget on ads shown to 100,000 strangers in the county.
Grows by
Emergent read
Mass, chasing reach without relationship. A hundred thousand impressions and almost no one who knows your name. When the money stops, so does the movement. brown would ask what those dollars would have grown if you had spent them on twelve real relationships instead.
2. Before launching anything public, an organizer spends three months just having coffee with fifteen local pastors, teachers, and shop owners.
Grows by
Emergent read
Connection, moving at the speed of trust. It looks like doing nothing. It is actually the whole foundation. Fifteen trusted people, each with their own network, is a base that can hold real weight later. This is the slow media of Week 2 as a growth strategy.
3. A cause has a warm, welcoming public feed. Inside the private group chat, the founder belittles volunteers and two quit every week.
Grows by
Emergent read
The tempting answer is "the public image is fine, so the cause is fine." Wrong. This is the fractal trap: the small-scale cruelty IS the movement's real character, and it will surface at scale. A cause that treats twelve volunteers badly cannot treat twelve thousand people well. The relationship layer is where it lives, and it is rotting.
4. A cause lets any local chapter act without asking headquarters first. One small chapter tries something odd; it unexpectedly reaches a huge audience.
Grows by
Emergent read
Mixed, and the point is the structure. Because it is decentralized, a small part could adapt and try something no central office would have approved, and the network got the upside. No single point of failure, and lots of small experiments running at once. This is how living systems find what works: many small tries, keep what survives.
5. A cause has 40,000 followers. When it calls a real meeting, five people show up.
Grows by
Emergent read
Mass without connection. The number looks like power and holds none. This is exactly the fragility Tufekci named in Week 10 and Dean named in Week 11: circulation that feels like a movement but cannot act. Forty thousand who watch is weaker than forty who will walk.
6. A cause hosts one deep eight-person story circle, then edits it into a short three-minute film that gets shared widely.
Grows by
Emergent read
Mixed, and this is the ideal. Something small and real (eight people telling the truth to each other) made first, then fractaled outward through fast media. The reach carries the character of the small thing, because there was a real small thing to carry. This is what you are building Thursday.

4 · A movement with no headquarters

Fayetteville, Arkansas, 2016

A woman named Jessica McClard built one small wooden box on a post outside a church, stocked it with food, and painted three words on it: take what you need, leave what you can. She called it a little free pantry. She did not start a nonprofit, launch a campaign, or hire anyone. She just built one good small thing and posted a photo of it.

People copied it. Not her organization, because there was none: they copied the pattern. A pantry in one town, then a hundred, then thousands, each one built and stocked and watched over by whoever lives nearby, in whatever wood and paint they had. No headquarters could shut it down because there is no headquarters. Cut any one node and the network is fine. It grew like mycelium, not like a franchise. The small unit already held the whole idea, so the whole idea could travel by being copied.

Your turn: the little free pantry had no central plan and spread anyway. What did growing by copying a small pattern give this movement that a single big national campaign never could? Name it in one sentence.
Compare with one sharp reading
"It gave every pantry a local owner who actually cares, so there's no single thing to defund or shut down, and the trust is built in because your neighbor made the box, not a logo." Decentralization plus a small, copyable pattern equals resilience. That is fractal growth in the wild.

Emergence

When a big, complex pattern arises from many small, simple interactions, with no one in charge of the whole. The parts follow local rules; the order appears on its own.

Example: a flock of starlings turning as one (no lead bird), or a market price, or a language. No one designs these from the top; they emerge from the small parts relating.

What emergence is →

adrienne maree brown

American writer, facilitator, and organizer (she lowercases her name). Her 2017 book Emergent Strategy argues that social movements should grow the way living systems do, small, adaptive, relational, decentralized, rather than the way ad campaigns do.

She draws heavily on the science-fiction of Octavia Butler and on the patterns of nature. This week is the philosophical turn of our course, and she is its voice.

Octavia Butler

Pathbreaking American science-fiction writer (1947–2006), one of the first Black women to win the field's top awards. Her Parable novels imagine communities surviving societal collapse by adapting and cooperating instead of dominating.

Why she's here: brown built much of Emergent Strategy on Butler's line "the only lasting truth is change," turning a novelist's vision into an organizing practice.

About Octavia Butler →

Fractal

A shape or pattern that repeats at every scale: zoom in or out and you see the same form again.

Example: a fern (each small frond is the shape of the whole leaf), a coastline, a branching tree, a snowflake. brown's version for people: "how we are at the small scale is how we are at the large scale," so a two-person conversation already carries the shape of the whole movement.

What a fractal is →

Mycelium

The root-like network of a fungus: a vast web of fine threads spreading underground through soil and connecting trees, trading water and nutrients. The mushroom you see is just the small fruit; the mycelium is the real, mostly-invisible organism.

Example: a single forest floor can hold miles of mycelium linking hundreds of trees, sometimes called the "wood wide web." brown uses it as the model for how movements should grow: relational, decentralized, patient, resilient.

An Appalachian note: these forests are some of the most fungally rich in the world. The old mountain practice of foraging (ramps, morels, chanterelles) depends on knowing that the mushroom is only the tip of a living network you never see.

Move at the speed of trust

One of brown's principles: relationships have their own pace, and you cannot force them faster. If a movement grows faster than the trust holding it together, it tears.

Example: asking a first-time follower to donate or organize is outrunning trust. A smaller first ask (come to one low-stakes thing) lets trust build until a bigger ask can hold.

Critical mass

A big number of people, loosely attached: followers, impressions, a crowd. It looks like power. It scatters the moment the attention moves on.

Example: 40,000 followers who like your posts but will not show up to anything. Real reach, almost no holding power.

Critical connections

brown's counter to critical mass: a smaller number of strong, real relationships that can bear weight and act. Build resilience by building relationships, not follower counts.

Example: twelve people who each know you and will show up at 6am when you call. That web holds under pressure where a crowd does not.

Decentralization

Spreading power and decision-making out across many small parts instead of one central command. No headquarters to capture, defund, or shut down; the parts can adapt on their own.

Example: a network where any local chapter can act without asking permission. Cut one node and the rest keep going. The trade-off is less top-down control, which is often the point.

What decentralization is →

Mutual aid

People in a community directly helping each other meet needs (food, care, money) as equals, without waiting on a charity or government. "Solidarity, not charity."

Example: the little free pantry, take what you need, leave what you can. No application, no gatekeeper, neighbors keeping neighbors fed.

An Appalachian note: this runs deep in the mountains, from old "workings" (a whole community showing up to raise a barn or bring in a harvest) to church pantries and pass-the-plate care. Small, trusted, decentralized help is one of the region's oldest strengths.

What mutual aid is →

Shape-note singing (Sacred Harp)

A very old style of communal singing where people sit in a hollow square facing each other and sing loud, unaccompanied four-part harmony from books that use shaped noteheads to teach the parts.

Why it's here: there is no audience and no single conductor. Singers take turns leading from the middle, and the point is the shared experience, not a performance. It is emergent, leaderless music: the whole sound comes from everyone relating in the moment.

An Appalachian note: shape-note and "singing convention" traditions are woven through Southern Appalachian church and community life, an all-day sing with dinner on the grounds. A room full of untrained neighbors making something powerful together, exactly the "social" this course keeps chasing.

What shape-note singing is →

Multimodal

Made of more than one "mode" of meaning at once: written language, image, sound, and design all working together. Most real messages are multimodal, and each mode does a job the others cannot.

Example: a short film uses words (the script), image (what you see), sound (voice and music), and design (how it is cut and paced). Change any one mode and the meaning shifts. This week you build your campaign in all four.

What multimodality is →

Vanity metrics

Numbers that look impressive and tell you almost nothing about real change: likes, follower counts, impressions. Easy to grow, easy to feel good about, disconnected from whether anything actually moved.

Example: a post with 50,000 likes and zero people who changed a behavior or joined the work. Week 14's whole job is telling these apart from metrics that mean something.
