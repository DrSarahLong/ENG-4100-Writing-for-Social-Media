Week 15 · Presentation prep

Present Your Campaign

The last move: you stand up and tell the room what you built for your cause, and what it actually did. About six minutes. Work through this before you present.
This is your prep, not busywork. Read it, build your talk in the planner, run the honest-metrics drill, then do the reflection. Keep your Field Notebook open and your cause in front of you.
Where the semester landed you: you picked one real, low-traction cause in Week 1 and carried it through a media analysis (P1), an audit and strategy (P2), and now a built campaign with a measured result (P3). Nobody in that room knows this cause the way you do. For your six minutes, you are the person who did the work. Own it.

1 · A talk is not your report read aloud

Your impact report is pages a reader can move through slowly. Your talk is six minutes an audience hears once, live. Different medium, different rules (you spent a whole semester on that idea). You cannot say everything, so stop trying. A strong campaign talk tells one clear story and lands one point.

The shape of it
Not "here is everything in my report, section by section." Instead: here is a cause you should care about, here is what it needed the media to do, here is the move I made and why, here is the work itself on the screen, here is what actually changed, and here is where it goes next. A story with a person in it, not a summary.

2 · The six beats

Every good campaign talk hits these, in order. The examples use a made-up cause (a push for a safe pedestrian crossing at a rural school) so you can see each beat land.

  1. The cause, with a face. Open so the room cares in ten seconds. A moment, a number, one real person. e.g. "Every morning, forty kids cross a fifty-mile-an-hour highway to get to Valle Crucis School. There is no crosswalk. There has never been one."
  2. The problem, and the media job. What did your cause need the media to actually do? Name the gap you found in P2. e.g. "Everyone local already agreed. The problem was not persuasion, it was that the county had never heard forty separate parents as one voice."
  3. The theory and the move. Which course idea drove your campaign, and why that one for this cause? e.g. "This called for relational organizing, not a viral post. A trusted parent asking another parent beats any ad, so I built a phone tree, not a hashtag."
  4. Show the work. Put your real artifacts on the screen. The reel, the zine, the flyer, the event photo. Let them see it rather than merely describe it. e.g. "Here is the one-page fact sheet parents handed to each other, and here is the sign-in sheet from the meeting it filled."
  5. Honest impact. What actually changed? Separate real movement from vanity metrics. Say what flopped too. That candor is part of the grade and the whole point of Week 14. e.g. "The Facebook post got twelve likes, which is nothing. But nineteen families showed up to the county meeting, and the commissioner put a traffic study on the agenda. Bodies in a room, not likes."
  6. What is next, and a close. The cause outlives this class. Name the next slow step, then one line that sticks. End clean, then "thank you." e.g. "The study runs in the spring. Forty kids still cross that highway tomorrow. So I am not done. Thank you."

4 · Slides that help, not hurt

The rule
Few words per slide. Your audience can read or listen, not both. Put your single strongest artifact up large: the reel plays, the zine spread fills the screen, the event photo shows real faces. A slide that needs a paragraph is a handout, not a slide. Show the work; let the room see the campaign was real.

5 · Honest impact or vanity metric?

This is the hard part of a campaign talk, and it is the whole lesson of Week 14. A number can look like success and mean almost nothing. Remember Jodi Dean: endless circulation can feel like politics while changing nothing. For each result below, make two calls. First, where does it sit: vanity, mixed, or real? Second, what does it actually tell you? Commit both, then Check. The honest answer is often "mixed."

Two judgments per result. The obvious read is sometimes wrong, a big number can be hollow and a tiny one can be real power.
1. Your launch reel hit 8,000 views.
Reads as
Tells you
Vanity, on its own. Eight thousand people scrolled past. You cannot reach any of them again, and not one has done anything yet. A view is the start of nothing until it turns into a relationship or an action.
2. Forty-two people signed up for your email list off that reel.
Reads as
Tells you
Mixed, leaning real. A list is not action yet, but now you own a way to reach forty-two interested people without renting it from an algorithm. That is worth more than the 8,000 views that fed it.
3. Your petition got 300 online signatures. Six people came to the town council meeting.
Reads as
Tells you
Mixed. The 300 signatures are circulation, a click costs nothing. The six bodies in the room are the real signal: those people acted. Dean's warning, live. In a room, six beats three hundred.
4. A local reporter called you for a story after your zine circulated at three churches.
Reads as
Tells you
Real. This is earned media: someone with a platform chose to act on your slow, high-trust work, no money changed hands. A reporter deciding you are worth covering is a person doing something, and it multiplies your reach for free.
5. A post got 1,200 likes, then engagement dropped to zero the next week.
Reads as
Tells you
Vanity. A spike that leaves nothing behind is rented attention: it stops the moment the algorithm moves on. If nothing carried over to the next week, the number measured the platform, not your cause.
6. Two volunteers grew into organizers who now run their own meetings without you.
Reads as
Tells you
Real, and the deepest kind. This is what adrienne maree brown means by growing capacity: the campaign now runs without you. No view count comes close to two people who can carry it forward.
Bring this into your talk: when you present your own numbers, do exactly this. Name what is vanity, name what is real, and say plainly what did not work. A presenter who admits the flop reads as more credible, not less. Evidence-honest is the whole brand of this course.

6 · Talk, do not read. Then practice out loud.

Reading a script word for word drains the life out of a talk and loses the room. Keep a few notes as reminders, look up, and talk to us like people. Know your first line and your last line cold, so you start and end strong even if the middle wobbles. Then rehearse the whole thing out loud, on a clock, at least twice. It is the only way to learn your real length (talks always run long) and to smooth the rough spots. Practicing in your head does not count.

A short Q and A follows each talk. You can prepare for it. Almost every campaign gets some version of "what would you do differently?", "what surprised you?", and "what is next for the cause?" Have a real answer ready for each. "Good question, I am not sure, but here is my guess" is a respectable answer. You do not have to know everything.

Ready to present?

  • I have one takeaway, and I say it out loud in the talk.
  • My strongest artifact is on a slide, big, so the room sees the real work.
  • I separate real impact from vanity metrics, and I name what flopped.
  • I can say it without reading from a script, first line and last line cold.
  • I practiced out loud and timed it. It is under six minutes.
  • I can answer "what would you do differently?" and "what is next for the cause?"

P3: Campaign + Impact Report

Your final project: a small, ethical, multimodal campaign for your cause, grounded in one course media theory, plus a report that measures and reflects on what it circulated and changed. The presentation is the spoken version of that report.

One takeaway

An audience forgets nearly everything in a short talk except, if you are lucky, one idea. So pick that idea on purpose and say it twice, near the start and again at the close.

Example: "A phone tree moved the county when a viral post could not." Say that line early, then end on it. Trying to deliver everything usually delivers nothing.

Vanity metrics

Numbers that look impressive but do not track what actually matters: likes, views, follower counts, impressions. They give the rosiest picture possible while telling you little about whether anyone acted, changed, or stuck around.

Example: a post with 5,000 likes and zero new volunteers is loud and empty. Twelve people who showed up to a meeting is quiet and real.

Jodi Dean · communicative capitalism

The political theorist you met in Week 11. Her warning: on social platforms, messages can become mere contributions to circulation, endlessly posted and forwarded while decoupled from any real action or response. Posting can feel like politics while changing nothing.

Why it is here: her lens is exactly how you tell a real result from a hollow one. If your number just added to the churn, it is circulation, not change.

Circulation vs. change

Circulation is a message moving around: shares, views, forwards, signatures. Change is someone doing something different because of it: showing up, giving money, shifting a policy, joining.

Example: 300 people sharing your petition is circulation. Six of them driving to the council meeting is change. A campaign talk should be honest about which is which.

Relational organizing

From Week 12: field research shows a real conversation with someone who knows you moves people that mailers and ads cannot. The most powerful medium is a trusted person.

Example: a parent texting another parent "will you come Tuesday?" outperforms any boosted post. If your campaign leaned on trusted messengers, this is the theory to name.

adrienne maree brown · emergent strategy

From Week 13: "small is good, small is all"; move at the speed of trust; grow like mycelium, not like an ad campaign. Critical connections over critical mass.

Why it is here: two volunteers who become organizers is her whole idea in one result. The deepest impact is capacity that keeps going without you.

Show one strong artifact

Put your single best piece of the campaign on a slide, large: the reel playing, the zine spread, a photo of real people at your event. A clear visual lands in three seconds what a paragraph of talking cannot.

Example: a full-screen photo of nineteen families at the council meeting says "this was real" faster than any sentence. Avoid text-heavy slides; the room cannot read and listen at once.

Talk, do not read

Reading a script flattens your voice and loses eye contact. Keep a few notes as reminders and speak to the audience. You know this cause better than anyone in the room; trust that.

Example: glance at "beat 3: the phone tree, why not a hashtag," then look up and just tell us. Talking to people beats a perfect paragraph read to the floor.

Practice out loud and time it

Rehearse the whole talk aloud at least twice, on a clock. It is the only way to learn your real length (talks always run long) and to smooth the rough spots before you are in front of people.

Example: your first run comes in at eight minutes; you cut a detail, not your takeaway, and land at five-forty. Practicing silently does not count. Your mouth needs the reps.

Handling questions

The Q and A is not a trap; people ask because they are interested. Prepare for the usual three, what you would do differently, what surprised you, and what is next for the cause, and you will field most of it.

Example: "What would I do differently? Skip the boosted post and put that time into the phone tree." Confident, specific, and clear about the flop. If you do not know, say so and offer your best guess.

Earned media

Coverage of your cause that a third party publishes without you paying for it: a reporter's story, a repost from a bigger account, a mention in someone else's newsletter. The opposite of a paid ad, and usually more trusted because you did not buy it.

Example: after your zine circulates at three churches, a local reporter calls and runs a story. You reached the whole county for free because your slow work earned it.
