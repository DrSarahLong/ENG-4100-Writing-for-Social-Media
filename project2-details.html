Step 1, in detail: the five elements of narrative

From Story-Based Strategy 101, developed by the Center for Story-based Strategy. Run all five on the dominant story, then run them again on the story you intend to tell.

Conflict
"The backbone of narrative, what defines the drama, point of view." Example, campus food insecurity: the circulating conflict is "students who budget badly versus a dining plan that already exists." Yours could be "a campus that charges for a meal plan most students cannot afford versus students who are hungry in class."
Characters
"Subjects, protagonists, and narrators of stories." Example: in the circulating story the protagonist is the administrator managing a budget and the student is a problem. In yours the protagonist is a junior working 28 hours a week, and the administration is a character who has a decision to make.
Imagery
"Words to capture imagination with metaphor, anecdote, and descriptions." Example: the circulating image is a swipe card. Yours is the shelf in the campus pantry at 4pm on a Thursday, empty.
Foreshadowing
"The ways that a story provides hints to its outcome." Example: the circulating story foreshadows nothing changing, because it presents hunger as a personal budgeting problem, which no policy can fix. Yours foreshadows a fix, because a swipe-donation program exists at three peer schools.
Underlying assumptions
"Unstated parts of the story that must be accepted." Example: the circulating story assumes a student who is hungry did something wrong. Name that out loud and it gets much harder to keep believing.

Step 3, in detail: the reframe

Worked example · a needle exchange facing closure "Needle program draws addicts downtown." Arguing back with "the program does not draw addicts downtown" repeats addicts and downtown and leaves the frame stronger than you found it. the frame you inherit, and the trap "Nobody who used this program died this year." Same program. A different frame entirely, built from a value nobody argues with, and it never touches their words. the frame you build

Step 4, in detail: Story of Self, Us, and Now

Worked example · a tenants' union fighting a rent hike Self. "The week I got the notice I sat in my car in the lot for an hour because I could not go inside and tell my daughter." Challenge: the notice. Choice: he organized instead of moving. Outcome: he knocked on 40 doors. Us. "There are 240 units in this complex and 190 of us got the same letter on the same day." Challenge: shared. Choice: shared. Outcome: shared. Now. "The rent board votes on the 14th, and they have never once heard from a tenant in this building." Challenge: the date. Choice: yours. Outcome: open.

Self without Us is a personal essay. Us without Now is a history lesson. Now without a specific ask is a complaint.

Step 5, in detail: audience by values

Chris Rose and Cultural Dynamics describe three broad values groups in What Makes People Tick: Settlers (security, belonging, the familiar), Prospectors (esteem, success, being seen), and Pioneers (ethics, complexity, the bigger picture). The same argument lands differently on each.

Worked example · one campaign, three versions of the same ask Settlers: "This is the block your grandmother walked. It should still be safe to walk it." Prospectors: "Three towns nearby already fixed this. We are the last one that hasn't." Pioneers: "The people who pay the most for this had the least say in it."

Step 6, in detail: the Story Canvas

One free page, eleven boxes, developed by Digital Storytellers and built on Alex Osterwalder's Business Model Canvas (CC BY-SA). You hand in a completed one alongside the strategy document. The boxes: Purpose (why tell this story, how things are now, what change you want) · Audience (who you want to reach, each segment named and profiled) · Story (how it starts, how it ends, what happens in between) · People & Places · Style & Tone · Key Messages (the three things they should remember) · Call To Action · Campaign (the touchpoints, on a timeline) · Outcomes · Indicators (how you measure it). Download it free →

Worked example · three indicators that are not follower counts Language: the student paper's next article on the pantry uses the word "cost" instead of "budgeting." Somebody else started telling your version. Behavior: 60 signatures, and 11 of those people show up to the board meeting. Showing up is a much higher bar than signing. Relationships: four people who had never met before the campaign now text each other without you in the thread.

What it needs

  • A diagnosis of the dominant story using all five elements of narrative, and the underlying assumption named explicitly.
  • A reframe that states the frame you refuse, explains why arguing with it backfires, and builds one from your own values.
  • A written Story of Self, Us, and Now, each part carrying a challenge, a choice, and an outcome.
  • One or two audiences named by what they value, and one call to action a person could finish this week.
  • A completed Story Canvas (all eleven boxes) with indicators that measure language, behavior, or relationships rather than follower counts.
  • Roughly 2,000 words plus the canvas, in a Google Doc someone else could tell your story from.
