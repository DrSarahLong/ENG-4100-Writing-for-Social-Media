1 · Vanity metrics and the honest ladder

A metric is a number you decide to watch. The trouble is that platforms hand you the easy numbers for free (views, likes, followers) and hide the ones that would actually tell you whether your cause is moving. A vanity metric is any number that reliably goes up and reliably tells you nothing you can act on.

See it: same number, different truth
"Our reel got 50,000 views." Sounds like a win. Now ask the follow-up questions: did anyone show up, sign, call, donate, or come back? If the answer is "we don't know," the 50,000 is reach and nothing more. Compare: "Nine people from that reel came to our first meeting and four are still volunteering." Smaller number, real impact, because it reached a rung the view count never touches.

Here is the ladder we built Tuesday. A metric isn't good or bad on its own. It's worth what it can reach. Rungs 1 and 2 are cheap and easy to fake. Rungs 3 through 5 are hard to get and hard to fake, which is exactly why they mean something.

5Powera decision changed; access to someone who couldn't be reached before
4Relationshippeople you can reach again; volunteers who stay; a list you own
3Actionsomeone did the thing: showed up, signed, called, switched, gave
2Engagementlikes, comments, shares, saves
1Reachviews, impressions: how many were shown it
Vanity lives on rungs 1–2. Impact lives on 3–5. The one test for any number: does it move something up to rung 3 or higher? If it can't, it's vanity, no matter how big.
Stay honest: rungs 1 and 2 are not worthless, and pretending they are will get you laughed out of a real strategy meeting. Reach is the fuel; the point is that fuel is only worth something if it drives the engine. A KPI (the metric you actually steer by) should almost always sit on rung 3 or higher. Watch reach the way you watch a gas gauge: useful, never the destination.
Read the real thing (6–8 min)

"Is Your Nonprofit Addicted to Vanity Metrics?" (Lean Startup, free, no login). Written for exactly your situation: a cause with a small budget and a big temptation to chase the numbers that feel good. Read it for the swap it keeps making, from a vanity number to an actionable one.

"Vanity metrics are those sneaky numbers that make you feel good about the work you've done, but don't get you closer to achieving your mission."
Your turn: name the one vanity metric your campaign is most tempted to lead with. Then write the actionable metric that should replace it (what you'd count instead, and how you'd count it).
Compare with one sharp reading
A strong answer names a specific replacement you can actually observe, not another feeling. Example: "Tempted to brag about follower count. Replace it with: RSVPs to our next in-person event, because a follower who won't cross the street for us isn't support, it's a number. I count RSVPs in the sign-up sheet, then count who actually shows."

2 · Circulation is not change

Week 11 gave you Jodi Dean and communicative capitalism: the trap where posting feels like politics because the message circulates, gets shared, quoted, liked, while never requiring a response from anyone with power and never requiring an action from you. The loop closes on itself. This is the theory under the vanity metric. A view is a contribution to circulation. Circulation is the thing that feels like change and isn't.

See it
A petition circulates to 10,000 signatures. Everyone who signs feels like they did something, and the feeling is real. If the target never has to respond and nothing follows, those 10,000 signatures did what Dean warned about: they turned a demand into content. The signing replaced the pressure it was supposed to create. The number went up. The needle did not.

So what should you watch instead? Zeynep Tufekci (Week 10) gives the sharpest answer. She argues that what actually threatens power isn't a movement's size on a given day, it's its capacity: the proven ability to act, to make decisions, to keep going, to escalate. A giant one-day march can signal big numbers while hiding the fact that the movement can't do anything next week. The metric that matters is not "how many showed up once" but "what can this group reliably do again." Behavior, relationships, and power, the top of the ladder, are just the visible signs of capacity.

Read the real thing (8–10 min)

Tufekci, Twitter and Tear Gas (the author's free full-text PDF, released under Creative Commons). Read the Introduction, and the section on how a big protest is a signal of underlying capacity, not the capacity itself. Ask as you read: what could this movement's numbers be hiding?

Your turn: describe a moment where your cause (or one you follow) had a burst of attention. Did it build capacity (people who can act again) or just circulate (a spike, then silence)? How can you tell the difference?
Compare with one sharp reading
A strong answer tests for what remains. Example: "The spike felt huge, but Tufekci's question exposes it: afterward we had zero new people we could contact directly. All the energy went into a feed we don't own. That's circulation. Capacity would have looked like 30 names in a spreadsheet we could email next month."

3 · Vanity or real?

Two judgments per result, and the honest answer is often "depends." First: is this a vanity number, a real sign of impact, or does it depend on something the sentence doesn't tell you? Second: what actually counts here? Commit both, then Check. The two cases that split the room are the point.
1. Your launch post is shown to 2 million people this week.
Call it
Counts as
Vanity. Two million impressions is rung 1, pure reach. Nobody did anything and you can't reach a single one of them again. Fuel, not a result, until it converts.
2. Fifty people showed up to a public meeting your campaign organized.
Call it
Counts as
Real. Fifty bodies in a room is rung 3, action. People spent a scarce, unfakeable resource (their evening) on you. That is worth more than any view count, and much harder to get.
3. Your account grew from 500 to 1,000 followers this month.
Call it
Counts as
Depends, and by default it counts as option 1. A follower is reach until you prove otherwise. If those 500 are local people you can turn out (rung 4), it's gold; if they're randoms or bought, it's decoration. The number alone can't tell you, which is exactly why "depends" is the honest call.
4. A city council member replied to your email and put your issue on the agenda.
Call it
Counts as
Real. This is rung 5, power. One reply from a decision-maker who now has to deal with you is worth more than a million strangers who never will. Access you didn't have last week.
5. Your petition reached 10,000 signatures. The target has ignored it and nothing has followed.
Call it
Counts as
Depends, and as described it's option 1, textbook Dean. Signatures are potential power (rung 5) only if you put them to work: deliver them loudly, organize the signers, threaten a real consequence. Ignored and unused, 10,000 signatures are 10,000 contributions to circulation.
6. Twelve volunteers now run your events on their own, without you in the room.
Call it
Counts as
Real, and the strongest one here. Rung 4, relationship and capacity. This is what Tufekci says power actually fears: a group that can act again next week whether or not you're watching. No view count comes close.

4 · The viral campaign that actually delivered

The ALS Ice Bucket Challenge, 2014

Everything about the Ice Bucket Challenge should have made it a vanity story: people dumped ice water on their heads, tagged three friends, and moved on. Peak circulation, low commitment, the exact shape of a stunt that changes nothing. Except it raised roughly $115 million for ALS research in about eight weeks, and the ALS Association put that money into research that helped identify a gene tied to the disease (NEK1). A viral moment converted into funded science and, eventually, changed lives.

So did the ladder just break? No. Look closer. The challenge had a conversion machine attached to the circulation: a specific ask (donate), an existing organization ready to catch and spend the money, and a clear behavior baked into the meme. The virality was fuel, and there was an engine to burn it in. That's the difference between this and KONY 2012, which had the reach and no working engine underneath.

Stay honest: this is the exception, and treating it as the rule is how campaigns die. Most viral moments have no engine and convert to nothing. The lesson isn't "go viral." It's that reach is only ever worth what it converts into, so build the engine (the ask, the list, the way to catch people) before you chase the reach.
Your turn: if a burst of attention hit your cause tomorrow, what's your engine? Name the one specific thing that would catch it and turn a view into an action. If you don't have one yet, say what you'd build.
Compare with one sharp reading
"Right now we have no engine, so a viral moment would evaporate. The one thing I'd build first is a pinned link to a 20-second volunteer sign-up form, so any spike drops names into a list I own instead of a feed I rent. Reach with nowhere to land is just weather."

Vanity metric

A number that reliably goes up and reliably tells you nothing you can act on. It flatters the effort without measuring the result.

Example: "Our post got 50,000 views." Impressive, and useless on its own, because it says nothing about whether a single person did anything. The fix is to swap it for a number that maps to an action.

What a vanity metric is →

Actionable metric

The opposite of a vanity metric: a number tied to a real behavior, so it can actually guide a decision. If it moves, you know something changed and you know roughly why.

Example: not "reach on our sign-up post," but "how many people completed the sign-up form." One is applause; the other tells you whether the post worked.

Reach vs. impressions

Reach is how many separate people saw something. Impressions is how many times it was shown, so one person who saw it five times is 1 reach and 5 impressions. Both live on the bottom rung of the ladder: they count eyeballs, not action.

Example: a post with 10,000 impressions and 2,000 reach was seen an average of five times each by 2,000 people. Neither number tells you if anyone cared.

What an impression is →

Engagement rate

The share of people who saw a post and did something light with it (liked, commented, shared, saved), usually written as those actions divided by reach or followers. It's a step up from raw reach, but still rung 2: a like costs almost nothing and commits no one.

Example: a post seen by 1,000 people with 50 likes and 10 comments has a 6% engagement rate. Better signal than views, still not a person in a room.

About engagement online →

KPI (key performance indicator)

The one or few numbers you decide to actually steer by, out of the hundreds you could track. Choosing your KPI is a values decision: it announces what you're really trying to do.

Example: if your campaign's KPI is "views," you'll make choices that chase attention. If it's "new volunteers who stay a month," you'll make completely different choices. Pick a KPI on rung 3 or higher.

Jodi Dean

Political theorist (Week 11). Her idea of communicative capitalism is the engine under this whole week: on our platforms, the endless circulation of messages can substitute for actual politics, so posting feels like doing something while the message just loops.

Her line for this week: a contribution to circulation is not an act. Your reach going up can feel exactly like organizing, and be nothing like it.

Communicative capitalism

Dean's name for the condition where our participation (posting, sharing, liking) gets captured as content and circulation rather than turned into decisions or responses from power. The activity is real; the political effect often isn't.

Example: a hashtag trends for a day, millions join in, no official ever has to answer for anything, and the trend fades. The circulating replaced the pressure.

Zeynep Tufekci

Sociologist of technology and social movements (Week 10, Twitter and Tear Gas). She studies why networked movements can gather huge crowds fast and still lose.

Her point for this week: a protest's size is a signal of underlying capacity, not the capacity itself. What power actually watches for is whether a movement can act again, decide, and escalate, none of which shows up in a view count.

Capacity

What a movement can reliably do, not how big it looked once. Can it turn people out again next month? Make a decision without falling apart? Escalate when ignored? Capacity is the real thing the top rungs of the ladder are trying to measure.

Example: 500 people at one march is a number. 30 people who will organize the next three actions whether or not it's trending is capacity, and it's scarier to power.

The ALS Ice Bucket Challenge

A 2014 viral fundraiser: people filmed themselves dumping ice water over their heads, challenged friends to do the same or donate to fight ALS (a fatal nerve disease), and posted it. It spread worldwide in weeks.

Why it's here: it raised roughly $115 million and funded research that helped find a gene tied to ALS. The rare viral moment with a real engine (a clear ask and an organization ready to spend the money) behind it.
