Why we are doing this

This is rehearsal for the room where someone decides whether to hire you, fund your cause, hand you a campaign, or admit you to graduate school. The people making those decisions are mostly Gen X and Boomers, and they learned a different set of presentation rules than you did.

Here is one gap in perception as an example:

Their side. In a December 2023 survey of 800 U.S. hiring managers, more than half said Gen Z candidates struggle most with eye contact in interviews, and 21% said some candidates refused to turn on their cameras in virtual interviews (Intelligent survey, reported by Fox).

Your side. In a 2023 survey of 1,015 Gen Z workers, 44% said they are uneasy or nervous about delivering presentations at work, and 42% named soft skills training as the top thing they want from employers (Kahoot! and Researchscape survey, reported by Agility PR).

You have spent a semester learning that some messages only land when a person is physically present. A presentation is that idea applied to you.

You have 8 to 10 minutes. By the end of today you will have: one takeaway sentence, a six-beat map of your talk, a staging plan for one or two physical artifacts, two planned audience questions, and a memorized first and last line.

Part 1 · Start with one sentence

A talk is not your æffect report read aloud. It is one idea, delivered so the room remembers it. Think of a TED or TEDx talk. The whole format is built on a single “idea worth spreading,” and the talks people remember can be summed up in a sentence. Yours should work the same way. Fill this in before you open Canva.

The basics

Who is listening?

Your classmates are in the room, but that’s not really your audience. Write for the person who could one day fund this cause or hire you to run a campaign. Picture someone your professor’s age in the back row who has never heard of your cause.

Part 2 · Map your six beats

These are the six beats from the Week 15 module, timed for your slot. Write keywords only, never full sentences. If you write sentences, you will read them, and the idea is to give a talk, not a reading.

1. The cause, with a face0:45

Make the room care in ten seconds: a moment, a number, or a person

2. The problem and the media job1:15

What your cause needed media to do, from your Project 2 audit

3. The theory and the move1:15

The course idea behind the campaign and why it fit this cause

4. Show the work2:30

Your artifacts on screen and your physical artifact in the room (Part 3)

5. Impact2:00

What changed, what was only attention, and what flopped

6. What’s next and the close1:00

The next slow step, takeaway again, one memorable last line, thank you

Your window is 8 to 10 minutes, talks run long, and going over reads as unprepared, which is why following this script should land you somewhere around 8 minutes 45 seconds.

Script only two sentences

Memorize these. Everything else you speak from keywords.

Sort your results for beat 5

List every result you could report, then label it. Views and likes on their own are usually vanity. Something a person did, a relationship you now have, or something that will outlast the semester is impact. “Mixed” is often the true answer.

Lead beat 5 with your strongest impact row, not your biggest number.

Say what flopped

Candid results are part of your grade, and in a workplace they are what makes people trust the rest of your numbers. Finish these out loud, without apologizing.

Part 3 · Stage your physical artifacts

You must bring in or build into your talk one or two physical artifacts from your campaign. This is the center of beat 4, and it is where you have an advantage over every slide deck the audience has ever sat through. An object in the room does what your campaign argued for: it requires someone to be there.

Remember the standard from Project 3. An artifact is something a person receives by being somewhere, holding something, or doing something with others. A photo of it is documentation, not the artifact.

Step 1: Choose

Pick the ones that carry your Project 2 story without explanation, that people can see or handle in under a minute, and that will survive the trip to class.

Step 3: If it cannot come to class

Chalk on a sidewalk, a procession, or a quilt on a wall will not fit in a backpack. Bring a piece of it: one numbered card, the stencil, a swatch, a signed letter. Then show one large photo and say that the photo is documentation.

Staging rules

  • Keep it out of sight until its moment. An object on the table from minute one is old news by minute five.
  • Reveal it, then stop talking for three full seconds. Let people look.
  • Hold it at chest height and keep it still. Do not wave it or talk to it. Look at the audience, not the object.
  • Passing one object around splits the room’s attention for minutes. Either bring enough for everyone, walk it past the front row yourself, or pass it and say only light things until it comes back.
  • Describe it out loud as you show it: size, material, what the text says. That is an access move, and it also helps the back row.
  • Never apologize for the craft. “It’s kind of messy” tells a decision-maker to discount it. Handmade is the point.
  • Tie it to your results. “Forty of these left the table. Eleven people came back with a friend.”

Presenting online? The artifact still has to appear

  • A slide of it does not count. Stop sharing your screen before you show it, so your video fills the window.
  • Turn off background blur and virtual backgrounds. They erase objects held up to the camera.
  • Hold it close to the lens, steady, for five full seconds. Rehearse this on camera so you know where the frame ends.
  • Describe what the camera cannot carry: weight, texture, size against your hand.
  • Then demonstrate what a person does with it. Unfold the zine. Put on the shirt. Sign the letter.
  • If you can, get one copy into the classroom ahead of time so someone in the room can hold it while you talk.
Why this matters past this class. Leaving something in a decision-maker’s hands is one of the oldest moves in pitching, and it is one your audience’s generation grew up with. A person holding your zine is still thinking about your cause after the slides are off.

Part 4 · Turn the audience into participants

The video we watched argues for building questions into your talk. A question changes the audience’s job from sitting through it to thinking with you. In an 8 to 10 minute talk, plan two and space them out. Each one should cost you under 30 seconds.

Type of questionExampleBest beat
Show of hands“Raise your hand if you have ever scrolled past a post about this.”1. The cause
This or thatShow two versions. “Which one would you stop for?”4. Show the work
Artifact prompt“Before you open it, what do you think is inside?”4. Show the work
Guess before the reveal“The reel got 1,900 views. How many people do you think showed up?”5. Impact
Seed the Q&A“The thing I still can’t explain is… I’d like your read on it.”6. Close

Rules that make questions work

  • Make it answerable in five seconds.
  • Ask, then wait three full seconds. Count them silently. It will feel long to you but normal to them.
  • Do something with the answer.
  • Have a line ready for silence.
  • Online presenters: tell people exactly how to answer. “Type 1 or 2 in the chat.”

Part 5 · Build it in Canva

Use Canva. It is free through the university, the templates handle fonts and spacing for you, and it runs in a browser on any classroom computer. Do not open it until Parts 1 to 4 are filled in. Slides come last because they support the talk. They are not the talk, and they are not your artifact.

Setup

Slide rules

  • One idea per slide. If a slide makes two points, it is two slides.
  • Six words is plenty. The audience cannot read and listen at the same time, and they will choose reading.
  • Show each digital artifact large, one per slide, filling the screen. No collages of six posts.
  • Add a plain or blank slide for the moment your physical artifact comes out, so the screen does not compete with it.
  • Type readable from the back row: 28 point or larger. No screenshots of an analytics dashboard. Pull out the one number and make it big.
  • Final slide: your takeaway and your campaign’s ask, not “Questions?” or “Thank you.” It stays on screen through the whole Q&A.

Part 6 · Say it so they listen

Writing for the ear is different from writing for a feed. A scroller can stop and reread. A listener cannot.

Write for the ear

  • Short sentences. One breath each.
  • Signposts. “Three artifacts. First…” tells the room where they are.
  • Make numbers human. “62%” becomes “nearly two out of three people who stopped at the table.”
  • One specific person or moment beats three general statements.
  • Repeat the takeaway. In writing that is redundant. Out loud it is how people remember.

Part 7 · End on purpose

The last thing you say is the thing the room carries out the door. Most talks waste it on “So, yeah, that’s it” or “Any questions?” A planned ending tells a decision-maker you were in control from the first word to the last. Your campaign had an ask. Your talk should end with the same nerve.

Type of endingHow it worksExample
Circle backReturn to the person, number, or moment from beat 1 and show what has changed“I started with an empty table at the market. Last Saturday, eleven people sat down at it.”
The takeawayYour one sentence from Part 1, slower and with nothing after it“A post got us seen. A zine in someone’s hand got us remembered.”
The askGive the room something to do, ideally with your artifact“Take one of these on your way out and leave it where a stranger will find it.”
The next slow stepSay what happens after the semester ends“Next month the table goes back out. Now you know where to find it.”

Rules for the last line

  • Make it a statement, not a question.
  • Keep it under 15 words so you can say it without looking down.
  • Say it, pause for two full seconds, then say “Thank you.” Questions come after that.
Never end on what flopped, your metrics, or an apology. The flop belongs in beat 5.

Draft three different endings, one line each. Do not settle for the first one.

Read all three out loud to a partner. Star the one they can repeat back, then copy it into My last line in Part 2.

Part 8 · If you draw an online slot

An online presentation is a job interview format now, so treat your slot as practice for one. Everything in Part 6 still applies. The screen removes most of your body language, which means what is left has to work harder.

Your delivery

  • Camera on for the whole talk. Camera off is the online version of presenting with your back to the room.
  • Look at the lens, not at faces on the screen and not at your own tile. Put a sticky note with an arrow next to the camera.
  • Sit tall or stand. Bring more energy than feels natural. The screen flattens you.
  • Slow down. Audio lags, and fast speech turns to mush.
  • For audience questions, tell people exactly how to answer: chat, reaction, or unmute.
  • If something breaks, say so calmly and keep going. “My slides froze, so I’ll show you the zine while they reload.” Recovering well impresses people more than a flawless run.
  • When you finish, stop sharing, stay on camera, and take questions.
When you are the audience. In the room: phone away, eyes up, handle the artifact with care, ask one question. Online: camera on, muted, responding in chat when asked. Presenters can see who is paying attention, and so can future colleagues.
