1 · Æffect: two words, because one was never enough

The Center for Artistic Activism, founded in 2009 by Stephen Duncombe and Steve Lambert, coined æffect for a problem they kept running into. Artists measure whether a thing moved people. Organizers measure whether a thing changed something. Creative activism has to answer both, and the two answers come from different evidence.

The two halves, on one campaign
Affect is what it moved in people: what they felt, said, remembered, or understood differently. Effect is what it changed in the world: a policy, a practice, a budget line, a decision, a count.

Take 200 hand-stamped cards collected over three weeks and carried into a school board meeting. Effect: the district published its supply-access plan nine days later. Affect: four people said it was the first time they had been in a room with their neighbors talking about it, and one of them cried while writing her card. The first one you can put in a table. The second one is why anybody came back the next week, and it will not fit in a table.
The complication: affect is the easier half to fake and the more difficult half to document, so the temptation runs both ways. “People seemed moved” is not evidence. A written quote with a date, a count of how many people stayed past the ask, a line somebody said that you wrote down within the hour: that is evidence. Collect affect the way you would collect anything else, in the moment, in writing.

2 · The ladder, and the column it cannot see

Here is the effect half, laid out. Any result you can produce sits on one of five rungs.

5Powera decision changed; access to someone who couldn't be reached before
4Relationshippeople you can reach again; volunteers who stay; a list you own
3Actionsomeone did the thing: showed up, signed, called, switched, gave
2Engagementlikes, comments, shares, saves
1Reachviews, impressions: how many were shown it
Vanity lives on rungs 1–2. Impact lives on 3–5. The one test for any number: does it move something up to rung 3 or higher? If it can't, it's vanity, no matter how big.

The ladder measures effect and nothing else. That is its use and its limit. A campaign can land on rung 3 and leave no trace in anybody, and a campaign can stall on rung 2 and change how a hundred people talk about the thing for a year. Your report has to carry both columns, because a creative campaign that only reports effect has thrown away its own advantage.

3 · Vanity or impact?

Six results from analog, handmade campaigns. Two judgments on each, and “depends” is frequently the right call. First: is this a vanity number, a sign of impact, or does it depend on something the sentence does not tell you? Second: what does it actually count as? Commit both, then Check.
1. Your photograph of the quilt square wall got 40,000 views on TikTok.
Call it
Counts as
Vanity. This is a number about the documentation, not the artifact. 40,000 people saw a photograph of a wall; nobody added to the wall because of it, and you cannot reach one of them again. Jodi Dean would point out that this is communicative capitalism doing exactly what the course has been warning you about.
2. Of 200 cards you put out over three weeks, 164 came back filled in.
Call it
Counts as
Impact, rung 3. Each of those 164 people stopped, picked up a pen, and wrote something in their own handwriting. That is a behavior with a cost, which is what makes it countable as action rather than attention. The 36 that did not come back are also information: report them.
3. Eleven people told you at the table that they loved the project.
Call it
Counts as
Depends, and by default it counts as option 1. Praise at a table is the analog version of a like: pleasant, cheap, and offered to your face by people who do not want to be rude. It becomes affect evidence if you wrote down what they actually said and when, and it becomes rung 3 only if one of them did something afterward.
4. The district published its supply-access plan nine days after your procession, and the published document uses the phrase your campaign built.
Call it
Counts as
Impact, rung 4, and the strongest result in this set. An institution did something it was not previously doing, and the language it used came from you, which is the language indicator from Project 2 showing up as evidence. Attribution is still a question you have to treat carefully: say what happened and when, and say what else was happening at the same time.
5. Six people asked how they could help with the next one.
Call it
Counts as
Depends, and it counts as rung 4 only if you got contact information. Six people who asked and walked away are six people you cannot find. Six names and numbers in a list you own is capacity, which is the thing Tufekci says power actually watches for. The difference between those two outcomes is a clipboard.
6. You made 60 buttons and all 60 were gone from the table by noon.
Call it
Counts as
Vanity, and this is the one that catches people, because it feels like distribution. Free things leave a table. You have measured supply, not interest, and you do not know whether a single button was worn. Count how many you see on backpacks the following week and you have a behavior instead.

4 · How to measure a handmade thing in three weeks with no money

You cannot run a validated survey, you have no sample frame, and you are not getting IRB approval by Week 16. What you can do is build the measurement into the artifact so taking part and being counted are the same act. The Center for Artistic Activism's own evaluation guidance leans on exactly these, and they are free.

The counts you already have

These are the cheapest actionable numbers you will ever get. Put out a known number and count what is left. How many taken, how many returned, how many filled in, how many stayed past the ask, how long the average person lingered.200 out, 164 back, 36 unaccounted for. Say the 36 out loud in the report.

A comment wall

The artifact collects the data. People write a line; the lines are the record, and you can code them later for language.Code the 164 cards for whether they use the word “charity” or the word “ready.” That is your language indicator, measured.

A speak-out, with somebody writing

An open mic or a two-chair session where one person's job is to write down what gets said, with the date. That is how affect becomes quotable.“I didn't know anybody else here was dealing with this.” Written down at 2:40 p.m. on the 14th, and used with permission.

Call and response

Ask the crowd a question with two physical answers, and count the bodies. A show of hands, a side of the room, a dot on a board.Two jars and a handful of beans: “Did you know the district has a program?” Yes jar, no jar. You now have a baseline.

An interactive prop

Something participants physically alter, so the artifact ends the week in a measurably different state than it started.A board with 240 blank squares, one per apartment. At the end, 83 are filled. The photograph of it is the finding.

The two-week follow-up

Copenhagen's best move, and the cheapest one. Go back to the same place two weeks later and ask five people whether they remember seeing anything.Three of five remembered it unprompted. One remembered the message and not the object, which tells you something about your design.
And the three indicators from Project 2 still apply
Language: did the words people use change? Collect a before sample and an after sample, with dates. Behavior: did anybody do something they were not doing? Count it. Relationships: are there people who will act again, and do you have their contact information? None of the three is a follower count, which is the point of having them.
Your turn: pick two of the six methods above and write exactly how you are running them on your own artifact. Include the number you expect and the number you would call a failure.
Compare with one strong reading
A strong answer commits to a number before it has the result. "CALL AND RESPONSE: two jars, one question, beans. 'Have you ever had to leave class to deal with this?' I expect the yes jar to be heavier, and if it is not, my premise about how common this is was wrong and that is worth finding out. COMMENT WALL: I code every card for whether it describes the problem as personal or as the school's. I expect most will say personal at the start of week one and I will compare week one against week three." Notice the second one builds a before-and-after comparison into a three-week table.

5 · Set the number before you run it, and report what failed

Two rules, and the rubric rewards both.

Decide what counts as success before the artifact goes out. A metric chosen afterward will always be the one you happened to hit. A metric you steer by, chosen in advance, can tell you no, which is the only reason to have one. Write the number down with a date next to it.
Report the part that did not work, in the report, in your own words. Not as a confession. As the finding that produces your iteration, which is æffect element 7 and five points of your grade.
What this sounds like when somebody does it properly

From the evaluation section of a campaign against a 40% rent increase at a 240-unit complex:

“Nine people spoke, not the eleven who had signed up. 140 of 200 zines went. The chalk was gone in one rain and almost nobody saw it, which was a design failure we should have predicted. The paper ran the story and used the word ‘increase.’ Affect: four people told us it was the first time they had been in a room with their neighbors, and one cried, which no metric caught.”

And the iteration that falls out of it: “Chalk was wrong for October. Next time the same information goes on a laminated card zip-tied to the mailbox cluster, where it stays dry and people stand still for thirty seconds anyway.”

Count the moves in that paragraph. A shortfall stated as a number rather than a feeling. A count with a denominator. A failure diagnosed rather than apologized for. One piece of effect evidence from outside the campaign. And one piece of affect evidence the author explicitly says the numbers missed.

Your turn: write your evaluation paragraph in that shape, using whatever you have so far. If your artifact has not run yet, write it in future tense with the numbers you are committing to.

Vanity metric

A number that reliably goes up and reliably tells you nothing you can act on. It flatters the effort without measuring the result.

Example: "Our post got 50,000 views." Impressive, and useless on its own, because it says nothing about whether a single person did anything. The fix is to swap it for a number that maps to an action.

What a vanity metric is →

Actionable metric

The opposite of a vanity metric: a number tied to a observable behavior, so it can actually guide a decision. If it moves, you know something changed and you know roughly why.

Example: not "reach on our sign-up post," but "how many people completed the sign-up form." One is applause; the other tells you whether the post worked.

Reach vs. impressions

Reach is how many separate people saw something. Impressions is how many times it was shown, so one person who saw it five times is 1 reach and 5 impressions. Both live on the bottom rung of the ladder: they count eyeballs, not action.

Example: a post with 10,000 impressions and 2,000 reach was seen an average of five times each by 2,000 people. Neither number tells you if anyone cared.

What an impression is →

Engagement rate

The share of people who saw a post and did something light with it (liked, commented, shared, saved), usually written as those actions divided by reach or followers. It's a step up from raw reach, but still rung 2: a like costs almost nothing and commits no one.

Example: a post seen by 1,000 people with 50 likes and 10 comments has a 6% engagement rate. Better signal than views, still not a person in a room.

About engagement online →

KPI (key performance indicator)

The one or few numbers you decide to actually steer by, out of the hundreds you could track. Choosing your KPI is a values decision: it announces what you are actually trying to do.

Example: if your campaign's KPI is "views," you'll make choices that chase attention. If it's "new volunteers who stay a month," you'll make completely different choices. Pick a KPI on rung 3 or higher.

Jodi Dean

Political theorist (Week 11). Her idea of communicative capitalism is the engine under this week: on our platforms, the endless circulation of messages can substitute for actual politics, so posting feels like doing something while the message just loops.

Her line for this week: a contribution to circulation is not an act. Your reach going up can feel exactly like organizing, and be nothing like it.

Communicative capitalism

Dean's term for the condition where our participation (posting, sharing, liking) gets captured as content and circulation rather than turned into decisions or responses from power. The activity happens; the political effect often does not.

Example: a hashtag trends for a day, millions join in, no official ever has to answer for anything, and the trend fades. The circulating replaced the pressure.

Zeynep Tufekci

Sociologist of technology and social movements (Week 10, Twitter and Tear Gas). She studies why networked movements can gather huge crowds fast and still lose.

Her point for this week: a protest's size is a signal of underlying capacity, not the capacity itself. What power actually watches for is whether a movement can act again, decide, and escalate, none of which shows up in a view count.

Capacity

What a movement can reliably do, not how big it looked once. Can it turn people out again next month? Make a decision without falling apart? Escalate when ignored? Capacity is what the top rungs of the ladder are trying to measure.

Example: 500 people at one march is a number. 30 people who will organize the next three actions whether or not it's trending is capacity, and it's scarier to power.

Æffect

A word the Center for Artistic Activism made by jamming affect and effect together, because creative activism has to be judged on both and the two are usually measured separately. The Center was founded in 2009 by Stephen Duncombe, a scholar of political culture, and Steve Lambert, an artist, after years of watching artists and organizers talk past each other.

Affect: what it moved in people. What they felt, said, remembered, or understood differently afterward.
Effect: what it changed in the world. A policy, a practice, a budget, a decision, a number.

Example: a hand-stitched banner carried into a council meeting gets the item put on the agenda (effect) and gets three strangers exchanging phone numbers in the parking lot afterward (affect). Report only the first and you have thrown away the reason you made it by hand.
